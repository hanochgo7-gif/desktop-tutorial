# Team C2 — Language pedagogy, CEFR, feedback and skills development

> **Method.** Desk research done on 2026-10-30. Search results were used only to *find* sources. Every citation [n] points to a source that was opened and read in this session. Most were opened with WebFetch: publisher pages, open-access PDFs, institutional repositories, and scholarly index records (OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar) carrying the published abstract and bibliographic data. Long open-access PDFs that WebFetch returned as binary (Jeon & Day; Webb et al.; Steiss et al.; Taylor; Mali; Settles et al.; the Krashen and Richards books) were saved by WebFetch and converted to text locally, so the full text could be read and quoted.
>
> **Two exceptions, stated openly.** The Council of Europe sites (rm.coe.int, coe.int, ecml.at) return HTTP 403 (Cloudflare) to both WebFetch and curl. The **CEFR Companion Volume (2020)** [1] and the **Manual for relating examinations to the CEFR (2009)** [2] were therefore read in full from Internet Archive copies of the official rm.coe.int PDFs, retrieved with curl because WebFetch cannot open web.archive.org. Page numbers are those printed in the PDFs.
>
> **Limitations.**
> - **Paywalls.** Wiley, Springer, SAGE, Elsevier and most Cambridge article pages were 403 or behind a login. For many classic papers (e.g. Norris & Ortega 2000; Spada & Tomita 2010; Li 2010; Kang & Han 2015) only the published abstract was read, not the full text. **Effect sizes are given only where the opened source states them.** For example, the commonly quoted numeric effects of Norris & Ortega, Li, and Kang & Han are *not* given here, because the opened records do not contain them.
> - **Secondary reporting.** Two classics, Mackey & Goo (2007) and Hanus & Fox (2015), could not be opened. They are reported through opened secondary sources ([10], [64]), and this is flagged where they appear.
> - **Missing evidence.** No peer-reviewed evidence was found on English pronunciation or intelligibility problems specific to **Hebrew-L1** speakers. No meta-analysis of **extensive listening** was found.
> - **Moving field.** Research on LLM tutors moves fast. Several key items are 2025–2026 meta-analyses read at abstract level, plus one preprint.
> - **Generalisability.** Much SLA evidence comes from lab studies, university students, short treatments and English as the L2. Each section flags lab-versus-classroom, short-versus-long-term and age limits.
>
> **How to read effect sizes here.** Plonsky & Oswald analysed 346 primary studies from 91 L2 meta-analyses. They conclude that Cohen's generic benchmarks (d = .2/.5/.8) **generally underestimate** the effects typical in L2 research, and they argue for field-specific interpretation [5]. A "large" d of 0.8 in an L2 study is therefore less impressive than the label suggests. Pre-post (within-group) effects run larger than treatment-versus-control effects [21][22][40].
>
> **Evidence-strength labels.**
> - **Strong:** several meta-analyses that agree.
> - **Moderate:** at least one meta-analysis, but with heterogeneity, mostly lab studies or few studies.
> - **Limited:** a few primary studies, vendor-authored work or preprints.
> - **Framework/opinion:** normative guidance or theory, not effect evidence.

## 1. CEFR — Companion Volume 2020, Pre-A1, writing, mediation, plurilingualism; assessing vs assigning levels

### Evidence summary

**What the 2020 Companion Volume (CV) is.**
- It updates the CEFR 2001, whose "conceptual framework … remains valid" [1, front matter].
- It replaces the 2001 descriptor set. The new set has:
  - a **new Pre–A1 level** and enriched A1 and C levels;
  - a **replacement scale for phonological competence**;
  - **new scales for mediation, online interaction and plurilingual/pluricultural competence**;
  - sign-language scales [1, back cover].

**It is a reference tool, not a standard.**
- "the CEFR is a tool to facilitate educational reform projects, not a standardisation tool. Equally, there is no body monitoring or even co-ordinating its use" [1, p. 28].
- It "does not prescribe any particular pedagogic approach". The CV adds: "There is, for example, no suggestion that one should stop teaching grammar" [1, p. 28].
- Its methodological message is action-oriented: learners are "social agents", and learning "should be directed towards enabling learners to act in real-life situations" [1, pp. 28–29].

**Pre-A1.**
- Pre-A1 covers simple, general tasks "scaled below Level A1" that "can constitute useful objectives for beginners". Examples include basic greetings, saying yes/no/please/thank you/sorry, and filling in forms with personal details [1, p. 36].
- It was developed from the Swiss Lingualevel and Japanese CEFR-J projects [1, p. 37].
- Example descriptors:
  - Written production: "Can give basic personal information (e.g. name, address, nationality), perhaps with the use of a dictionary" [1, p. 66].
  - Online interaction: "Can post online short simple statements about themselves … provided they can select them from a menu and/or refer to an online translation tool" [1, p. 85].
- Pre-A1 has **no descriptors** on several scales, such as reports and essays [1, p. 68].

**Written production up to C2 (the argumentative-writing target).**
- "Reports and essays" starts only at A2, with "No descriptors available" at A1 and Pre-A1.
- **B2:** "Can produce an essay or report which develops an argument systematically with appropriate highlighting of significant points and relevant supporting detail."
- **C1:** "Can expand and support points of view at some length with subsidiary points, reasons and relevant examples." Producing an introduction and conclusion to a longer text is qualified with "provided … there are opportunities for redrafting and revision."
- **C2:** "Can set out multiple perspectives on complex academic or professional topics, clearly distinguishing their own ideas and opinions from those in the sources" [1, p. 68].
- At B2, "Overall written production" adds "synthesising and evaluating information and arguments from a number of sources" [1, p. 66].

**Real-life functions by level** (Appendix 1, salient features).
- **A2:** most "social functions" (greetings, invitations, short social exchanges) and "getting out and about" (transactions, "get simple information about travel", public transport).
- **B1:** "deal with most situations likely to arise when making travel arrangements … or when actually travelling".
- **B2:** "sustain relationships with users of the target language without unintentionally amusing or irritating them".
- **C2:** "a good command of idiomatic expressions and colloquialisms with awareness of connotative levels of meaning" [1, pp. 173–174].

**C2 is not native-speaker competence.** C2 "has no relation whatsoever with what is sometimes referred to as the performance of an idealised 'native speaker'" [1, p. 36]. The replacement phonology scale makes **intelligibility** the key level discriminator, not accent (see §7) [1, p. 132].

**Mediation.** "the user/learner acts as a social agent who creates bridges and helps to construct or convey meaning, sometimes within the same language … and sometimes from one language to another (cross-linguistic mediation)" [1, p. 90].

**Plurilingual competence.** Plurilinguals have "a single, interrelated, repertoire" that is "uneven and changing" [1, p. 29].

**Levels vs profiles.**
- "Levels are a necessary simplification … any simple answer like B2 – or even B2 receptive, B1 productive – hides a complex profile." The many scales exist "to encourage users to develop differentiated profiles" [1, p. 37].
- Plus levels (e.g. A2+) mark "a very strong competence at a level that does not yet reach the minimum standard for the next criterion level" [1, p. 37].

**Descriptors in assessment.**
- The main function of descriptors is "to help align curriculum, teaching and assessment" [1, p. 41].
- Activity descriptors ("what" a learner can do) suit teacher and self-assessment of real-world tasks. Competence descriptors ("how well") feed assessment criteria and grids [1, pp. 42–43].
- Self-assessment lists work better when short: "much shorter (for example, 10 to 20 descriptors)", against 60–80 for a curriculum level [1, p. 42].

**Assigning vs assessing a level (CoE Manual 2009).**
- Linking an exam to the CEFR is "a process of 'building an argument'" through five procedures: **Familiarisation, Specification, Standardisation training/benchmarking, Standard setting, Validation** [2, p. 9].
- Start with productive skills, "since these can be more directly related to the rich description in the CEFR" [2, p. 9].
- "Linking of a test to the CEFR cannot be valid unless the examination or test … can demonstrate validity in its own right" [2, p. 9].
- "B2 represents a band of language proficiency that is quite wide": two exams both "at B2" may set their pass marks at different points [2, p. 4].

### Strength of evidence

**Framework/guidance.**
- The CV descriptors were scaled and validated in a large project [1, front matter]. The CV makes no claims about teaching *effectiveness*, and none should be inferred.
- The Manual is procedural guidance for making defensible level claims.

### What it means for Lucid

- Use CV descriptors, including Pre-A1, mediation and online interaction, as the **outcome spine**.
- Report a **per-skill profile**, not one global level.
- Label results as "estimated, CEFR-aligned". Claiming a *certified* CEFR level would require the Manual's linking procedures.
- Pre-A1 must be a real on-ramp: survival phrases, forms and menu-based online posting.
- The C1–C2 writing track maps directly onto "Reports and essays" (multiple perspectives, distinguishing one's own views from sources, redrafting).

## 2. Comprehensible input (Krashen) and its critiques

### Evidence summary

**Krashen's input hypothesis (1982).**
- Understanding input containing i + 1 is "a necessary (but not sufficient) condition" for moving from stage i to i + 1.
- If input is understood "and there is enough of it, i + 1 will automatically be provided". Hence "the best input should not even attempt to deliberately aim at i + 1".
- "speaking fluency cannot be taught directly. Rather, it 'emerges' over time, on its own" [6, ch. II].
- In the later preface, Krashen cites the "inefficacy of error correction" as confirmed [6, preface]. That claim is directly contested in §8–9.

**Critiques and the current reading.**
- Lichtman & VanPatten note that Monitor Theory "was widely criticized and dismissed" as theories came to be judged by stricter criteria.
- They argue that three hypotheses survive under new names [7]:
  - acquisition/learning becomes **implicit vs explicit learning**;
  - natural order becomes **ordered development**;
  - the input hypothesis becomes **a central role for communicatively embedded input**.
- This is a position essay, not new data.

**Empirical tests of "input only".**
- The Shintani, Li & Ellis meta-analysis compared comprehension-based (input) and production-based (output) grammar instruction (30 studies, 35 projects) [8]:
  - Both had **large** effects.
  - Comprehension-based instruction was better for **receptive** knowledge on immediate tests, but the gap narrowed on delayed tests.
  - For **productive** knowledge, the two were similar short-term, but **production-based instruction was more effective on delayed tests**.
- This contradicts the strong claim that production need not be practised.

**Input does drive incidental vocabulary learning, but slowly.**
- Across 24 studies (N = 2,771), mean proportions of target words learned incidentally were 9–18% on immediate tests and 6–17% on delayed tests [9].
- Reading, listening and reading-while-listening gave similar gains. Viewing gave smaller ones [9].

### Strength of evidence

- Input as necessary: **strong consensus** [7][9].
- Input as *sufficient*: **not supported**. Meta-analytic evidence favours output practice for durable productive knowledge [8], and explicit instruction adds value (§5).

### What it means for Lucid

- Every unit needs **plenty of comprehensible, meaningful input**, both text and audio.
- Input-only design (passive watch/read apps) leaves productive skills, which are central to the writing lab and roleplay, under-trained [8].
- Incidental uptake of roughly 10–15% per encounter context [9] means vocabulary needs **deliberate recycling** (SRS) as well as input.

## 3. Output and interaction hypotheses

### Evidence summary

**Theory** (as summarised in a state-of-the-art review [10]).
- **Long's interaction hypothesis:** negotiation for meaning after a communication breakdown is "posited as the driving force" for comprehension and L2 development [10].
- **Swain's (comprehensible) output hypothesis:** output is a causal factor in development, not just a reflection of it. It serves four functions: noticing, hypothesis testing, metalinguistic reflection, and fluency/automaticity [10].

**Meta-analytic evidence.**
- Mackey & Goo (2007), not opened and reported via [10], meta-analysed **28 interaction studies**. They found **large effects** for interaction versus no interaction, "seen more clearly on delayed posttests" [10].
- Loewen & Sato conclude that interaction is effective but that "many factors" moderate it: interlocutor, task, linguistic target and context [10].

**Computer-mediated interaction.**
- Ziegler's meta-analysis of 14 studies found **no significant difference** between text-based synchronous computer-mediated and face-to-face interaction in developmental benefit [11].
- There was a small SCMC advantage on written productive measures and a small face-to-face advantage on oral and receptive outcomes [11].
- These are **human–human** chats, not chats with an AI.

**Output for productive knowledge.** See Shintani et al. [8] above.

### Strength of evidence

**Moderate to strong** for interaction overall [10]. Caveats:
- Much of the interaction evidence is lab-based [10].
- Evidence comes from human interlocutors; transfer to AI interlocutors is assumed, not shown (see §11).

### What it means for Lucid

- AI roleplay should create **genuine negotiation for meaning**. It should ask for clarification when the learner's meaning is unclear rather than silently "understanding" everything. Learners should have to *produce* language, not just choose options.
- Text chat is a legitimate interaction mode [11]. Add voice mode for oral outcomes.

## 4. Task-based (TBLT) and communicative language teaching (CLT)

### Evidence summary

**TBLT programmes.**
- Bryfonski & McKay's meta-analysis of 52 studies reported a "positive and strong effect (d = 0.93)" for TBLT programmes, with positive stakeholder perceptions [12].
- This estimate has been **seriously challenged**:
  - Boers, Bryfonski, Faez & McKay (including the original authors) re-examined it with two other meta-analyses. They warned that results depend on effect-size calculation, variable definition and study eligibility, and that replication attempts "produced results that differ from all three originals" [13].
  - Boers & Faez argue that, with one exception, the 27 comparative studies did not suit the purpose [14].
  - A re-analysis by Xuan et al. (reported in [14]) of 16 studies, redefined as *task-supported* programmes, found a **smaller** pooled effect.
  - Boers & Faez conclude that the field "is not yet ready" for meaningful meta-analyses of task-based *or* task-supported programmes, partly because what counts as a "task" is unclear [14].

**Critique of strong TBLT.**
- Swan argues that the claim that grammar is acquired mainly through incidental noticing during tasks rests on "unproved hypotheses".
- In his view, TBI is good for consolidating known language but "considerably less effective for the systematic teaching of new language", especially where time is limited and out-of-class exposure is low [15]. That is an opinion paper.

**CLT.**
- Richards' Cambridge booklet summarises CLT's methodological principles [16]:
  - make real communication the focus;
  - let learners experiment;
  - tolerate errors as signs of developing competence;
  - develop *both* accuracy and fluency;
  - integrate skills;
  - let learners induce or discover grammar.
- Littlewood notes that CLT was questioned, especially in Asian contexts. It now functions as an **umbrella term** for approaches that develop communicative competence meaningfully, rather than as a single method [17].

### Strength of evidence

- Task-based programme superiority: **contested**. The headline d = 0.93 should not be quoted [12][13][14].
- CLT: **framework/expert opinion**, with no single efficacy estimate.

### What it means for Lucid

- Use a **task-supported** design: every unit ends in a real-world task (book a room, argue a position, comment on a post).
- Tasks are prepared by explicit input and practice, especially at A-levels for self-study adults with little exposure [15].
- Do not market "task-based" as a proven-superior method [14].

## 5. Explicit vs implicit instruction

### Evidence summary

- **Norris & Ortega (2000)**, 49 samples from studies published 1980–1998 [18]:
  - Focused L2 instruction gave **large** target-oriented gains.
  - **Explicit instruction was more effective than implicit.**
  - Focus on Form and Focus on Forms had equivalent, large effects.
  - Effects were durable.
  - The outcome-measure type affected effect sizes, and generalisability was limited by poorly operationalised constructs.
- **Spada & Tomita (2010)**, 41 studies [19]:
  - Explicit instruction showed **larger effects than implicit** for both simple *and* complex English grammatical features.
  - It contributed to both controlled knowledge and **spontaneous use**.
  - This answers the worry that explicit benefits show only on test-like measures.
- **Kang, Sok & Han (2019)**, 54 studies, 5,051 learners, 1980–2015 [20]:
  - Overall instruction effect g = 1.06 (95% CI 0.84–1.29).
  - Only a **"minor difference"** between explicit and implicit instruction.
  - Significant moderators: outcome-measure mode, learners' starting proficiency, research setting and intensity.

### Strength of evidence

**Strong** that form-focused instruction works [18][19][20]. **Moderate** that explicit beats implicit: two meta-analyses favour explicit, while the most recent large synthesis finds only a small difference [20].

Known limits:
- Older meta-analyses relied heavily on controlled, test-like outcome measures [18].
- Most studies are short.
- Effect sizes are inflated relative to Cohen's benchmarks [5].

### What it means for Lucid

- Give **brief explicit explanations**, available on demand and kept short, for every grammar target, inside a communicative unit.
- Measure success on **free production**, such as roleplay and writing tasks, not only gap-fills. Otherwise Lucid will over-estimate its own effectiveness [18][20].

## 6. Extensive reading and extensive listening

### Evidence summary

**Extensive reading (ER).**
- Jeon & Day (2016): 71 samples from 49 studies, N = 5,919 [21].
  - ER versus control: d = 0.57 (95% CI 0.46–0.68). Pre-post: d = 0.79. The authors call both "small to medium".
  - Moderators [21]:
    - adults d = 0.70, children 0.52, adolescents 0.35;
    - EFL 0.65 vs ESL 0.38;
    - web texts 0.89 vs paper 0.47;
    - ER **as part of the curriculum** 0.91 (k = 11), ER in a reading course 0.47 (k = 38), ER as a stand-alone reading course 0.24 (k = 1).
  - Programme length did not matter (0.51/0.59/0.60).
  - ER worked better "when it was accompanied by other classroom activities such as interactive vocabulary instruction, group discussion, or oral presentation" [21, p. 258].
  - Several cells rest on one study, and the authors urge caution [21].
- Nakanishi (2015): 34 studies, N = 3,942. d = 0.46 for group contrasts and 0.71 pre-post [22].
- ER is defined as reading "a lot of easy material" [21, p. 246, quoting Bamford & Day].

**Extensive listening.** **No meta-analysis was found** in this session.
- The closest evidence is Webb et al.'s incidental-vocabulary meta-analysis [9]:
  - listening (≈15% of targets immediately, 13% delayed);
  - reading-while-listening (13%/17%), similar to reading (17%/15%);
  - viewing lower.

### Strength of evidence

- ER: **moderate to strong**. Two meta-analyses agree on medium effects. Many studies are Asian-Pacific EFL samples, and subgroup cells are thin [21].
- Extensive listening: **limited** (indirect evidence only).

### What it means for Lucid

- Build a **graded reader/listening library at every level from A1**, with audio for reading-while-listening.
- **Integrate it into the unit flow** with light follow-up (discussion prompts, vocabulary capture) rather than leaving it as a separate optional shelf [21].
- Default recommendations should be **easy** texts (at or below the learner's level).

## 7. Pronunciation: intelligibility vs nativeness; computer-assisted pronunciation training (CAPT)

### Evidence summary

**Accent ≠ unintelligibility.**
- Munro & Derwing (1995): 18 native-English listeners rated speech by 10 Mandarin speakers.
  - The speech was largely intelligible and comprehensible, even though accent ratings ranged widely.
  - "a strong foreign accent does not necessarily reduce the comprehensibility or intelligibility of L2 speech" [23].
- Derwing & Munro (2005) propose a framework "in which mutual intelligibility is the primary consideration", while noting the social side of accent. They point to functional load and CALL as research priorities [24].
- The CEFR CV *replaced* its 2001 phonology scale because "the focus on accent and on accuracy instead of on intelligibility has been detrimental". Intelligibility is now the key discriminator between levels [1, p. 132].

**Instruction works.**
- Lee, Jang & Plonsky (2015), 86 reports [25]:
  - d = 0.89 within-group and 0.80 between-group;
  - larger effects with longer interventions, **with feedback**, and on **controlled** outcome measures.
- Thomson & Derwing (2015), narrative review of 75 studies [26]:
  - Most studies still targeted native-like pronunciation.
  - Segmentals were studied more than suprasegmentals.
  - Read-aloud tasks dominated assessment and "very few studies measured spontaneous speech".
  - So transfer to real talk is under-evidenced.

**ASR-based CAPT.** Ngo, Chen & Lai (2024), 15 studies, 38 effect sizes [27]:
- Overall g = 0.69 (95% CI 0.31–1.08).
- Explicit corrective feedback g = 0.86 vs indirect (e.g. ASR dictation) g = 0.50.
- Segmental g = 0.82 vs suprasegmental g = 0.37.
- Duration: 1–4 weeks g = 0.07 (negligible); 5–8 weeks g = 1.01; 9+ weeks g = 0.72.
- Practising alone g = 0.44 vs with a peer g = 0.89. **Both subgroup CIs cross zero.**
- Adults g = 1.20.
- Many subgroups rest on two to four studies [27].

**Hebrew-L1 specifics.**
- No peer-reviewed study of Hebrew speakers' English intelligibility was found.
- One relevant finding: skilled Hebrew readers, shaped by an orthography where a letter often maps to a consonant-vowel unit, were **slower to isolate initial phonemes**. They tended to delete whole CV units [4]. This suggests explicit phoneme-level awareness work for beginners learning English letter–sound mapping (an inference, not tested on learners).

### Strength of evidence

- Pronunciation instruction works: **strong** [25].
- Intelligibility as the right target: **framework plus experimental support** [1][23][24].
- ASR feedback: **moderate** (small k, wide CIs) [27].
- Hebrew-specific priorities: **no direct evidence**.

### What it means for Lucid

- Feedback on **intelligibility** ("clear / hard to understand, because …"), never "accent score" or "% native".
- ASR practice should give **explicit, segment-level feedback** and be **spaced over at least 5 weeks** [27].
- Include **spontaneous-speech** tasks, not only read-aloud [26].
- Build any Hebrew-specific contrast list from Lucid's own data before hard-coding it.

## 8. Oral corrective feedback (CF)

### Evidence summary

**Lyster & Ranta (1997).** 18.3 hours of immersion classroom data [28].
- Teachers overwhelmingly used **recasts**, "in spite of the latter's ineffectiveness at eliciting student-generated repair".
- Elicitation, metalinguistic feedback, clarification requests and repetition led to student-generated repair more successfully [28].

**Brown (2016).** Meta-analysis of classroom CF *distribution* [29]:
- recasts = 57% of all CF;
- prompts = 30%;
- grammar errors receive the most CF (43%).

**Li (2010).** 33 studies [30]:
- **medium** overall effect, **maintained over time**;
- implicit feedback's effect was *better maintained* than explicit feedback's;
- lab studies > classroom studies;
- shorter > longer treatments;
- foreign-language > second-language contexts;
- published studies did not show larger effects than dissertations.

**Lyster & Saito (2010).** 15 *classroom* studies, N = 827 [31]:
- CF had "significant and durable effects";
- **prompts > recasts**;
- effects largest on **free constructed responses**;
- younger learners benefited more than older ones.

### Strength of evidence

**Strong** that oral CF helps [30][31]. **Moderate** on which CF type is best:
- prompts beat recasts in classrooms [31];
- implicit feedback is better maintained in a mixed lab/classroom sample [30].

Lab effects exceed classroom effects [30]. No study in this set tested an AI interlocutor's CF.

### What it means for Lucid

- In roleplay and tutoring, default to **prompts** (elicitation, metalinguistic clue, clarification request) for the unit's **target forms**, so the learner self-repairs.
- Use recasts for non-target errors only when they impede meaning.
- Keep CF light enough not to break the conversation. CF frequency caps are a **design assumption**, not an evidence-derived number.

## 9. Written corrective feedback (WCF): the Truscott debate; focused vs unfocused

### Evidence summary

**The debate.**
- Truscott (1996) argued that grammar correction in L2 writing "should be abandoned", for three reasons [32]:
  - research shows it ineffective;
  - theory predicts it to be ineffective;
  - it has harmful effects.
- Ferris (1999) replied with "The case for grammar correction" [33].
- Bitchener (2008), "Evidence in support of written corrective feedback", studied **direct** CF on the English article system with low-intermediate learners (from index keywords; abstract not available) [34].

**Meta-analysis.**
- Kang & Han (2015), 21 studies: WCF "can lead to greater grammatical accuracy". Its efficacy is mediated by learner proficiency, setting and genre of the writing task [35].

**Comprehensive (unfocused) WCF.**
- Van Beuningen, De Jong & Kuiken (2012), N = 268 [36]:
  - Both direct and indirect comprehensive CF improved accuracy beyond self-editing or extra writing practice.
  - This held for revisions **and new texts** written 1 and 4 weeks later.
  - **Only direct CF** improved *grammatical* accuracy in new writing. Indirect CF most helped *non-grammatical* accuracy (e.g. spelling, lexis).
  - CF did **not** lead learners to simplify their writing, contrary to a Truscott prediction [36].
- Reynolds & Kao (2022), 34 studies of unfocused feedback [37]:
  - effective on immediate posttests, with apparently growing benefits on delayed tests;
  - but only 12 studies had delayed data, and effects were heterogeneous.

**Focused WCF.**
- Abalkheel & Brandenburg (2020), a small synthesis of 10 quasi-experiments [38]:
  - focused feedback had positive effects;
  - comprehensive feedback "could have a harmful effect … over time";
  - WCF in general was inconclusive.

### Strength of evidence

- WCF improves accuracy: **moderate to strong** [35][36]. Truscott's blanket "abandon it" position is not supported by later syntheses.
- Focused vs unfocused: **unresolved**. Syntheses point in different directions [36][37][38], and results depend on proficiency and setting [35].

### What it means for Lucid

- Correct, but **make the format a tested variable**: focused versus comprehensive, by level.
- Use **direct** correction for grammar and **indirect** (located and coded) correction for non-grammatical errors [36].
- Always follow with **revision and a new text** to check transfer [36].

## 10. Automated writing evaluation (AWE)

### Evidence summary

**Fleckenstein, Liebenow & Meyer (2023).** 20 studies, 84 effects, N = 2,828 [39]:
- Overall automated feedback effect g = 0.55 (95% CI 0.19–0.91).
- The **95% prediction interval runs from −1.02 to 2.12**, so in some contexts the effect may be negative.
- None of the subgroup differences were statistically significant:
  - L2 learners g = 0.72 vs L1 0.40;
  - interventions of more than two sessions g = 0.66 vs one or two sessions 0.18 (not significant);
  - transfer (learning) outcomes g = 0.65 vs revision (performance) 0.27 (not significant);
  - follow-up g = 0.27 vs post-test 0.57.

**Ngo, Chen & Lai (2024), EFL/ESL only.** 24 studies / 85 between-group effects and 34 studies / 178 within-group effects [40]:
- between-group g = 0.59 ("medium"); within-group g = 0.98;
- larger for vocabulary than grammar;
- "Grammarly shows potential … though Pigai did not demonstrate such effectiveness";
- medium-to-long durations beat short ones;
- larger for undergraduates, EFL settings and intermediate learners.

**LLM feedback quality (Steiss et al. 2024).** 200 secondary essays, human vs ChatGPT (GPT-3.5) feedback [41]:
- Humans were "slightly better … in all categories other than criteria-based".
- AI had more low-end outliers ("more likely [to] offer inaccurate feedback").
- Feedback quality did not differ by English-learner status.
- Ratings could not be truly blinded [41].

**LLM scoring (Mizumoto & Eguchi 2023).** GPT-3 scored 12,100 TOEFL11 essays with "a certain level of accuracy and reliability", useful to *support* human evaluation [42].

### Strength of evidence

**Moderate** for AWE on L2 writing [39][40]:
- high heterogeneity;
- short-term-heavy;
- mostly university samples;
- tool-specific results;
- little evidence on retention.

LLM-based AWE: **limited and early** [41][42].

### What it means for Lucid

- AWE can help, but only as **repeated draft–feedback–revise cycles over weeks**, judged on **new-text transfer** [39][40].
- AI scores should be presented as estimates with uncertainty, calibrated against human-rated anchors.

## 11. AI chatbots and LLM tutors (2023–2026): benefits and risks

### Evidence summary

**Benefits.**
- Wang, Cheung, Neitzel & Chai (2025), *Review of Educational Research*, 28 studies, 70 effects: positive overall effect **g = 0.484** versus non-chatbot conditions [46].
  - Moderators: educational level, language level, interface design, interaction capability.
- Lee, Lee & Lee (2026), a multilevel meta-analysis of **speech-enabled LLM chatbots**, 19 studies, 32 samples, N = 2,643 [47]:
  - effect on L2 learning outcomes **0.68**;
  - effect on affective outcomes **0.64**;
  - moderated by proficiency, educational level, interaction format and context of use.
  - Read at abstract level only.

**State of the research.**
- Lo et al. (2024) reviewed 70 empirical ChatGPT ESL/EFL studies. Most focused on writing, and few quantitatively measured effects on performance [48].
- Reported risks: **incorrect information**, privacy leakage and academic dishonesty. Reading, speaking and listening are under-researched [48].

**Over-reliance.**
- Bastani et al. (2025, PNAS): a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high-school *maths* students [49].
  - GPT-4 access improved practice performance by 48% with a ChatGPT-like interface and 127% with a tutor with safeguards.
  - When access was removed, the ChatGPT-like group did **17% worse** than students who never had access. Students used it "as a 'crutch'".
  - Learning-protective prompts ("GPT Tutor") largely mitigated the harm [49].
  - Not an L2 study.
- Kosmyna et al. (2025, **preprint**): 54 participants writing essays [50].
  - The LLM group showed the weakest neural connectivity and the lowest sense of essay ownership, and struggled to quote their own essays.
  - Not peer-reviewed and not an L2 study.

**Hallucinated or over-eager corrections.**
- Fang et al. (2023) found ChatGPT's grammatical error correction fluent but prone to **over-correction**, not following minimal edits [43].
- Davis et al. (2024) tested 10 LLMs on 4 benchmarks. LLMs do **not consistently beat supervised grammar-correction systems**, and outperform them mainly on fluency-rewrite benchmarks rather than minimal-edit ones [44].
- Mali (2026) systematically reviewed 23 studies of ChatGPT WCF in EFL/ESL [45]. ChatGPT can give inaccurate WCF that is:
  - irrelevant;
  - misreads intent;
  - changes meaning;
  - adds unnecessary items;
  - makes sentences more complex;
  - and is "greatly influenced by how prompts were presented".

### Strength of evidence

**Moderate** for short-term benefits [46][47]. The evidence base is young, mostly short interventions, often confounded with novelty, and heavily writing-focused [48].

**Moderate and growing** for over-reliance and accuracy risks:
- one strong RCT outside L2 [49];
- one preprint [50];
- consistent evaluation studies on over-correction [43][44][45].

### What it means for Lucid

- An AI tutor is worth building, but **with guardrails as a core feature**:
  - hints before answers;
  - attempt-first;
  - minimal-edit corrections with explanations;
  - unassisted checkpoints;
  - visible uncertainty.
- Measure learning without the AI, not only performance with it [49].

## 12. Evidence on app-based learning efficacy (and who funded it)

### Evidence summary

**Vendor-commissioned work.**
- Vesselinov & Grego (2012) was **commissioned and funded by Duolingo** [51][52]. The headline result: "34 hours to learn the equivalent of a first college semester's worth of Spanish" on WebCAPE.
- TechCrunch also reported that "many participants dropped out or only studied for two hours" [51].
- Jiang et al. (2021, *Foreign Language Annals*): 225 Duolingo completers reached ACTFL Intermediate Low in reading and Novice High in listening, "comparable" to fourth-semester university students. **No speaking or writing was assessed** [53].
  - Four of five authors were Duolingo employees [53].
- Taylor (2022) critically reviewed Duolingo's in-house reports [55]:
  - The researchers "were almost entirely Duolingo employees".
  - Samples were triply self-selected: of 8,367 course completers, 813 answered, 499 were eligible and 195 started the test.
  - 90% of one sample had at least a bachelor's degree.
  - The "only learning tool" claim did not screen out other input.
  - Taylor also credits third-party tests and transparency [55].
- Loewen, Isbell & Sporn (2020): a Babbel evaluation (54 learners, 12 weeks, about 10 min/day) found gains in grammar, vocabulary and oral ability. **One co-author was affiliated with Babbel** [54]; no funder is listed in the index record.

**Independent evidence.**
- Loewen et al. (2019): an independent semester-long Duolingo case study (n = 9, Turkish) found gains, plus "variability in motivation" and "frustration with instructional materials" [52].
- Shortt et al. (2023) reviewed 35 Duolingo studies (from 367 records). Most were design-focused and quantitative and used **non-probability sampling** [56].
- Mihaylova et al. (2022), a meta-analysis of 23 app studies (N = 1,873) [57]:
  - g = 1.08, or 0.88 without outliers;
  - but **all studies had high risk of bias** and GRADE quality was low to very low;
  - only 3 studies had delayed posttests.

### Strength of evidence

**Limited.** Apps can produce gains in receptive skills and vocabulary. Comparisons like "equals a semester" rest on self-selected, vendor-authored samples. Productive-skill and long-term evidence is thin [53][55][57].

### What it means for Lucid

- Lucid must not repeat the "X hours = Y semesters" style of claim.
- Any efficacy claim needs an **external proficiency measure, a comparison group or a credible counterfactual, attrition reporting, delayed testing and disclosed funding**.

## 13. Gamification and motivation (self-determination theory)

### Evidence summary

**Self-determination theory (SDT).**
- Three basic needs, **competence, autonomy and relatedness**, sustain self-motivation and well-being when satisfied. When thwarted, motivation and well-being decline [58].
- Noels et al. (2000) validated SDT-based intrinsic/extrinsic motivation scales for L2 learning [60].

**Rewards.**
- Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999), 128 experiments [59]:
  - engagement-contingent rewards **undermined** free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = −0.40);
  - completion-contingent rewards: d = −0.36;
  - performance-contingent rewards: d = −0.28;
  - **positive feedback enhanced** free-choice behaviour (d = 0.33) and interest (d = 0.31).

**Gamification effects.**
- Sailer & Homner (2020) [61]:
  - cognitive g = .49 (k = 19), motivational g = .36 (k = 16), behavioural g = .25 (k = 9);
  - only the cognitive effect stayed stable in methodologically rigorous studies;
  - game fiction and social interaction moderated behavioural outcomes, and **competition combined with collaboration** worked best.
- Bai, Hew & Huang (2020): 30 interventions, N = 3,202, g = 0.504 (95% CI 0.284–0.723) on academic performance [62].

**Harms.**
- Hanus & Fox (2015), not opened and reported via [64]: a gamified course with badges, leaderboard and coins showed **lower intrinsic motivation, satisfaction and empowerment and lower final exam scores** than a non-gamified course [63][64].
- Balci et al. (2022) found no harm from badges and leaderboards in their own experiments. However, 31.3% of leaderboard-only students agreed that rank comparison was discouraging (Exp. 1), and there is "no consensus" on leaderboards [64].

### Strength of evidence

- **Moderate** for small-to-medium average gamification benefits [61][62]. Results are heterogeneous, and motivational effects are unstable.
- **Strong** that expected, engagement-contingent rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation [59].
- **Limited but credible** evidence that leaderboards can harm some learners [63][64].
- No peer-reviewed evidence on **streaks** was found in this review.

### What it means for Lucid

- Reward **evidence of learning**, not time-on-app.
- Use **informational, competence-building feedback** (can-do progress).
- Support autonomy with real choices.
- Keep competitive features **opt-in** and pair them with collaboration.
- Treat streaks as an unproven mechanic to be tested, not assumed.

## 14. Placement testing and adaptive testing (CAT) basics

### Evidence summary

**How the Duolingo English Test works** (Settles, LaFlair & Hagiwara 2020) [65].

*Model.*
- IRT with a **Rasch model**: P(correct) = 1 / (1 + exp(δᵢ − θ)), where δ is item difficulty and θ is ability.
- Items and scores sit on a **CEFR-anchored 0–100 scale** (A1 = 0 … C2 = 100).
- Item difficulties were **predicted by ML/NLP models** instead of costly human pilot testing.

*Adaptive procedure.*
- The first items are sampled from ascending difficulty bins (a "calibration" phase). After four items, a provisional θ selects each next item's difficulty.
- The test stops at **25 items or 40 minutes**, whichever comes first.
- "Examinees are best evaluated by items where δᵢ ≈ θ."

*Reported quality.*
- Internal consistency .96 (n = 9,309); test–retest .80 (n = 526).
- Correlations with TOEFL iBT and IELTS r > .7.
- With added writing and speaking sections, test–retest rose to .85 and correlations to .77/.78 [65].
- This is a vendor-authored, peer-reviewed paper (TACL).

**Self-assessment for placement.**
- Ross (1998) meta-analysed 60 correlations. Self-assessment validity coefficients **vary**, and validity depended on learners' experience with the skill being rated [3].
- So self-assessment is a weak sole placement instrument but a usable prior.

**Level claims.** See §1. A placement test does not become "CEFR-certified" by using CEFR labels. Linkage needs the Manual's five procedures and a test valid in its own right [2].

### Strength of evidence

- CAT/IRT: **established psychometric practice** with **published validation** for a vendor test [65].
- Self-assessment: **meta-analytic but old** [3].

### What it means for Lucid

- Use a short Rasch-based CAT for receptive placement, plus brief productive samples.
- Treat placement as **provisional** and refine it from performance data.
- Never present it as a certified CEFR result.

### B. Lesson and unit design

| ID | Decision (testable) | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| D13 | **Unit template.** Meaningful input → brief explicit focus (≤ 90 s to read, **design assumption**) → controlled practice → free production/interaction task → feedback → retrieval. A unit fails schema validation if it lacks either an input step or a free-production step. | [6][7][8][18][19] |
| D14 | **Every productive target gets ≥ 1 production task**, and its mastery is checked on a **delayed** production item (≥ 1 week later, **design assumption on interval**). | [8] |
| D15 | **Mastery is measured on free production.** For each grammar target, at least one assessment item is free production (roleplay turn or written sentence in context), not only a gap-fill. | [18][19][20] |
| D16 | **Task-supported, not strong task-based.** Every unit ends in a real-world task (the Real-Life track: travel, social, slang). At A-levels the task is preceded by explicit language support. Lucid makes **no** marketing claim that "task-based is proven superior". | [12][13][14][15][16] |
| D17 | **Roleplay negotiation for meaning.** The AI partner is instructed to ask for clarification or confirmation when the learner's utterance is ambiguous or unintelligible, rather than inferring intent. QA: a test set of ambiguous learner turns must produce a clarification request in ≥ 80% of cases **(design assumption on threshold)**. | [10][11] |
| D18 | **Integrated extensive reading/listening.** A graded library with audio for every text from A1. **Every unit links ≥ 1 library text** with a light follow-up (discussion prompt or vocabulary capture). Recommendations default to texts at or below the learner's reading level. The weekly default target (e.g. 3 × 15 min) is a **design assumption** to be A/B-tested. | [21][22][9] |
| D19 | **Vocabulary from input** is captured into the SRS deck on learner request. Lucid does not assume incidental learning alone will suffice (expected incidental uptake is roughly 10–15%). | [9] |

### C. Feedback in roleplay, tutor and writing lab

| ID | Decision (testable) | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| D20 | **Oral CF policy (default).** For the unit's target forms: first give a **prompt** (elicitation, metalinguistic clue or clarification request). If the learner does not self-repair after one prompt, give the correct form with a one-line explanation. For non-target errors, give a recast only when meaning is impeded. Cap: **≤ 1 correction per learner turn**, plus an end-of-session summary of **≤ 3 error patterns** (caps are **design assumptions**). | [28][29][30][31] |
| D21 | **Learner-controlled CF intensity** (off / light / standard / intensive), default *standard*. The setting is stored and respected by the roleplay, tutor and writing lab. | [58] (autonomy) |

Default until results arrive: focused at A2–B1, learner-toggleable comprehensive at B2+ **(design assumption)**. | [35][36][37][38] |

### D. AI tutor and LLM guardrails

| ID | Decision (testable) | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| D26 | **Minimal-edit correction.** The LLM correction prompt and post-processor enforce minimal edits. A correction that changes meaning, adds content or rewrites more than the error span is rejected. Style suggestions are shown separately and labelled *optional*. QA metric: on a held-out learner-text set, edits outside the gold error spans stay below a pre-set rate (**design assumption on threshold**). | [43][44][45] |
| D27 | **Every correction is explainable and checked.** Each correction carries an error category and a one-sentence explanation, and passes a second, independent check (rule-based grammar checker or a second model pass). If the checks disagree, the correction is shown as "uncertain" or suppressed. | [41][43][44][45] |
| D28 | **Human audit.** A monthly random sample of AI corrections and AI writing scores is rated by trained teachers. Accuracy (precision) is tracked per error category, and categories below target are switched to "suggestion" mode. The sample size and target precision are **design assumptions**. | [41][42][45] |
| D29 | **AI writing scores** are shown as a CEFR-descriptor band with an uncertainty range, never a single precise number. They are calibrated before launch against human-rated anchor essays per level. | [42][2] |
| D30 | **Attempt-first tutor.** On graded exercises and writing tasks, the tutor gives hints in steps (hint → targeted clue → worked explanation) and never the full answer or full essay before the learner has made an attempt. A "write it for me" request in the writing lab is refused, with scaffolding offered instead. | [49] |
| D31 | **Unassisted checkpoints.** At the end of each unit there is a short AI-free check (tutor disabled). Analytics track the assisted vs unassisted performance gap per learner. A large gap triggers more independent practice (the threshold is a **design assumption**). | [49][50] |
| D32 | **Transparency.** Tutor and feedback views say "AI feedback can be wrong — tap to report". Reports are logged and feed D28. | [41][45][48] |
| D33 | **Speech-enabled conversation practice** is included, with outcomes measured (pre/post speaking task) rather than assumed from meta-analyses that are mostly short-term and novelty-prone. | [46][47][48] |

## What not to build

1. **A "CEFR certificate" or "official level"** from Lucid's own placement test. It cannot be valid until a Manual-style linking study is done [1][2].
2. **A single global level number** without a per-skill profile [1].
3. **An input-only "just listen and absorb" product** with no production practice or explicit help. Durable productive skill is better supported by output practice and explicit instruction [8][18][19].
4. **A strong-TBLT curriculum for beginners with no explicit language support**, marketed as "proven". The headline effect is contested [13][14][15].
5. **"Accent score", "% native-like" or "accent-reduction" features or copy.** The target is intelligibility [1][23][24].
6. **Pronunciation assessed only by read-aloud** [26].
7. **An AI tutor that hands over answers or writes the essay.** Unguarded access can harm unassisted performance [49].
8. **LLM "corrections" that rewrite the learner's text** or are shown without explanation and checks. LLMs over-correct and can be wrong [43][44][45].
9. **Red-penning every error in every roleplay turn**, or recast-only correction. Prompts work better for self-repair, and conversation flow matters [28][31].
10. **XP or rewards for time-on-app, global leaderboards by default, or paid streak repair** [59][63][64].
11. **Efficacy marketing built on in-house, self-selected samples or engagement metrics** [51][55][57].
12. **Self-assessment as the sole placement method** [3].
13. **Hard-coded "Hebrew speakers' mistakes" lists presented as evidence.** No peer-reviewed basis was found; collect data first.
