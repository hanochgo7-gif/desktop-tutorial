# Team C2 — Language pedagogy, CEFR, feedback and skills development

> **Method.** Desk research done on 2026-10-30. Search results were used only to find candidate sources. Every citation [n] points to a source opened with WebFetch in this session. These were publisher or repository pages, open-access PDFs, preprint pages, or scholarly index records (OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, Crossref) that carry the published abstract and bibliographic data. Some open-access PDFs came back from WebFetch as binary. Those were saved by WebFetch and converted to text locally with `pdftotext` so the full text could be read: the CEFR Companion Volume, Krashen, Jeon & Day, Nation, Kremmel et al., Ngo et al., Settles et al., Steiss et al., Taylor, Broderick, Izumi and Hosogoshi.
>
> **Limitations, stated openly.**
> - **CEFR source.** The Council of Europe domains (coe.int, rm.coe.int) returned HTTP 403 to WebFetch. The **CEFR Companion Volume (2020)** [1] was therefore read in full from a university-hosted mirror of the official PDF. The mirror carries the Council of Europe imprint, the PREMS 058220 code and the original page numbers. The 2009 *Manual for relating language examinations to the CEFR* could not be opened. It is described here only through the Companion Volume's own account of it.
> - **Paywalls.** For many classic papers only the published abstract or index record was read, not the full text. This applies to Norris & Ortega 2000, Spada & Tomita 2010, Li 2010, Lyster & Saito 2010, Kang & Han 2015, Sailer & Homner 2020 and Wang et al. 2024. **Effect sizes are reported only where the opened source states them.** For example, no numeric effects are given for Norris & Ortega or Li 2010, because the opened records do not contain them.
> - **Records without findings.** For a few items, only the bibliographic record could be opened, with no usable abstract. These are Mackey & Goo 2007, Truscott 2007, Ellis et al. 2008 and Lichtman & VanPatten 2021. They are cited only for their existence and scope, never for findings. Hanus & Fox (2015) is reported through the Semantic Scholar record's machine-generated summary and is flagged as weak.
> - **Sources that could not be opened at all.** These include Swain (1985, 1995), Long (1996), Hu & Nation (2000), Derwing & Munro (2005), Thomson & Derwing (2015), Levis (2005), the Vesselinov & Grego (2012) report itself, the Montero Pérez et al. (2013) captioning meta-analysis and Chang & Millett (2014). Where they matter, they are reported via an opened secondary source, and this is stated.
> - **Missing evidence.** No peer-reviewed source on English pronunciation or writing difficulties specific to **Hebrew-L1** learners was opened. No meta-analysis of **extensive listening** was opened.
> - **Generalisability.** Much SLA evidence comes from lab studies, university students, short treatments and English as the L2. One feedback meta-analysis found lab effects larger than classroom effects [14]. Effect size depends on the outcome measure, and more controlled measures give larger effects [11][48]. Findings on treatment length conflict: [14] found shorter treatments gave larger effects, while [15] and [48] found longer ones did. Vendor-authored research is flagged as such.
>
> **Evidence-strength labels.**
> - **Strong:** several meta-analyses agree in direction.
> - **Moderate:** at least one meta-analysis, but with heterogeneity, mostly lab studies, few studies, or contested methods.
> - **Limited:** a few primary studies, vendor-authored work or preprints.
> - **Framework/theory:** normative guidance or theoretical claims, not effect evidence.

## 1. CEFR Companion Volume 2020: global scale, Pre-A1, written production and interaction, mediation, plurilingual competence; how levels should be assessed vs assigned

### Evidence summary

**What the Companion Volume (CV) changes.**
- The CV updates the 2001 CEFR and gives a complete set of descriptors that **replaces the 2001 set** [1, back cover]. The additions include:
  - a new **Pre-A1** level and enriched description at A1 and at the C levels;
  - a **replacement scale for phonological control**;
  - new scales for **mediation**, **online interaction** and **plurilingual/pluricultural competence**;
  - modality-inclusive, gender-neutral wording [1, back cover].
- Some descriptors that referred to accommodation by "native speakers" were changed, "because this term has become controversial" [1, Table 2, p. 24].

**Pre-A1.**
- Pre-A1 is a "band of proficiency that is halfway to A1". Descriptors for it are provided "for many scales, including for online interaction" [1, Table 2, p. 24].
- It was developed from the Swiss Lingualevel and Japanese CEFR-J projects, which targeted primary and lower-secondary school [1, p. 38]. The CV does not describe any separate validation of Pre-A1 with adults. This is our inference from those origins, not a statement in the CV.
- Typical Pre-A1 descriptors depend on support:
  - Overall written production: "Can give basic personal information (e.g. name, address, nationality), perhaps with the use of a dictionary" [1, p. 66].
  - Written interaction: can convey basic information "in short phrases on a form or in a note, with the use of a dictionary" [1, p. 82].
  - Online conversation: can post short simple statements about themselves "provided they can select them from a menu and/or refer to an online translation tool" [1, p. 86].
- Even at **A1**, online postings are expected to rely "on the aid of a translation tool" [1, p. 86].

**Global scale.**
- The common reference levels table covers A1 to C2. It has no Pre-A1 row [1, p. 175].
- A1: "Can understand and use familiar everyday expressions and very basic phrases aimed at the satisfaction of needs of a concrete type…".
- B1: "Can produce simple connected text on topics which are familiar or of personal interest…".
- C1 adds "clear, well-structured, detailed text on complex subjects, showing controlled use of organisational patterns, connectors and cohesive devices" [1, p. 175].

**Written production toward argumentative writing.** The "Reports and essays" scale [1, p. 68] is the most direct anchor for Lucid's C1–C2 writing goal.
- **A1 and Pre-A1:** "No descriptors available".
- **B1:** short, simple essays, and listing advantages and disadvantages to justify an opinion.
- **B2:** an essay or report "which develops an argument, giving reasons in support of or against a particular point of view", and the writer can "synthesise information and arguments from a number of sources".
- **B2+ (above the plus-level line):** the argument is developed "systematically with appropriate highlighting of significant points and relevant supporting detail", and the writer can evaluate different ideas or solutions to a problem.
- **C1:** clear, well-structured expositions of complex subjects. Notably, it also includes producing an introduction and conclusion to a longer text "provided … there are opportunities for redrafting and revision".
- **C2:** "can set out multiple perspectives on complex academic or professional topics, clearly distinguishing their own ideas and opinions from those in the sources".

**Written interaction** progresses from formulaic notes at A2 to expressing oneself "in an appropriate tone and style in virtually any type of formal and informal interaction" at C2 [1, p. 82].

**Mediation.** "the user/learner acts as a social agent who creates bridges and helps to construct or convey meaning". This can happen within one language, across modalities, or "from one language to another (cross-linguistic mediation)" [1, p. 90].

**Plurilingual competence.** The scale "Building on plurilingual repertoire" progresses in three stages [1, p. 127]:
- At the A levels, the learner exploits "all possible resources" to handle a simple everyday transaction.
- At B2, the learner alternates flexibly between languages.
- At the C levels, the learner can gloss sophisticated abstract concepts in different languages.

Users are invited to complete a descriptor by naming the languages concerned, in place of the generic wording [1, p. 127]. The CV's own example names "English, Spanish and French". A Lucid version would name Hebrew and English. That wording is ours and is not quoted from the CV.

**Phonology.** The 2001 phonology scale was "the least successful" of those calibrated. The CV states that "the focus on accent and on accuracy instead of on intelligibility has been detrimental". The new scale uses intelligibility, meaning "how much effort is required from the interlocutor to decode the speaker's message", as "a key factor in discriminating between levels" [1, p. 133]. The new descriptors still refer to accent explicitly at every level, as the influence of other languages [1, p. 133]. Accent is described, but it is not the criterion that separates levels.

**Assessing vs assigning levels.** The CV separates descriptors from assessment instruments, and level from profile.
- The CEFR's suggested assessment criterion is "communicative ability in real life, in relation to a continuum of ability". This is "the original and fundamental meaning of 'criterion'" in criterion-referenced assessment [1, p. 29].
- The CEFR is "a tool to facilitate educational reform projects, not a standardisation tool", and "there is no body monitoring or even co-ordinating its use" [1, p. 29].
- For examinations, the Council of Europe points to its *Manual for relating language examinations to the CEFR*, a toolkit, an ALTE test-development manual, and ECML's RELANG capacity-building [1, p. 29, notes 23–26]. That is, linking a test to CEFR levels is a separate, procedural standard-setting task.
- The descriptor scales "are not intended to be used as assessment instruments, though they can be a source for the development of such instruments". They are "illustrative", "open-ended and incomplete" [1, p. 41].
- A descriptor's level "should not be seen as exclusive or mandatory". It marks the first level at which a learner is most likely to be able to do the task [1, p. 41].
- Descriptor lists for teacher or self-assessment work better when short: "10 to 20 descriptors", against "60 to 80" for a whole-level curriculum [1, p. 43].
- Two kinds of descriptor serve different purposes [1, p. 43]:
  - Activity descriptors ("can do") suit teacher or self-assessment of real-world tasks.
  - Competence descriptors are the source for quality criteria. Assessment grids "usually restrict themselves to four or five assessment criteria".
- Levels are "a necessary simplification". Any single label such as "B2" "hides a complex profile", and real profiles are uneven [1, p. 38].
- "Plus levels", such as B1+, represent "a very strong competence at a level that does not yet reach the minimum standard for the next criterion level" [1, p. 38].
- "It has never been the intention that the CEFR should be used to justify a gate-keeping function of assessment instruments" [1, foreword].
- The CV's sample proficiency profile uses the bands Pre-A1, A1, A2, A2+, B1, B1+, B2, B2+ and C1 (and C2 in its plurilingual profile). It spans oral and reading comprehension, oral and written interaction, oral and written production, and mediation [1, p. 40]. It has no A1+ band.
- The descriptors themselves were scaled empirically. In the original Swiss project, Rasch analysis was used on 250 teachers' ratings of learners [1, p. 41]. This calibrates the *descriptors*; it does not test teaching methods.

### Strength of evidence
**Framework/guidance.** This is authoritative normative guidance with empirically scaled descriptors. It is not evidence about which teaching method works. Pre-A1 descriptors were derived from school-age projects [1, p. 38].

### What it means for Lucid
- Lucid can say a lesson **targets** a descriptor. A **CEFR level is a claim that needs evidence**: performance on calibrated tasks, judged against criteria, with any formal "you are B2" claim requiring standard-setting.
- Progress should be reported as a **profile** across reception, production, interaction and mediation, with plus levels. It should not be a single badge.
- At Pre-A1/A1, Hebrew glosses, menus and a translation aid are **within the CEFR's own descriptors**. They are not "cheating".
- Mediation (Hebrew ↔ English) and plurilingual descriptors legitimise structured Hebrew use in tasks. Examples: summarising a Hebrew notice in English for a visitor, or explaining an English email to a Hebrew-speaking parent.
- The C1 descriptor's explicit mention of "redrafting and revision" supports a **multi-draft writing lab**.

## 2. Comprehensible input (Krashen) and its critiques

### Evidence summary
**Krashen's claims.**
- Understanding input that contains "i + 1" is "a necessary (but not sufficient) condition" to move from stage i to stage i + 1 [2].
- If the acquirer understands input "and there is enough of it, i + 1 will automatically be provided". The best input "should not even attempt to deliberately aim at i + 1" [2].
- Speaking fluency "cannot be taught directly. Rather, it 'emerges'" [2].
- In the online edition's preface, Krashen states that later research confirmed "the superiority of comprehensible-input based methods" and "the inefficacy of error correction", citing Truscott [2].

**Critiques with direct relevance.**
- **Output.** Swain's Output Hypothesis was "formulated in reaction to Krashen's" claim, based on Canadian immersion research [3] (secondary report of Swain 1985). Immersion produced excellent comprehension and fluency. Yet learners, "even after many years in these programs, still fail to achieve high levels of performance in some morpho-syntactic aspects" [3]. Swain attributes this partly to too little production.
- **Explicit instruction works.** Meta-analyses find large, durable effects of focused instruction, with explicit instruction outperforming implicit [11][12]. This contradicts a strong reading of "learning does not become acquisition".
- **Error correction works, at least orally.** Oral corrective feedback shows a medium overall effect that is maintained over time [14], and "significant and durable effects" in classrooms [15]. This contradicts the preface's blanket claim about error correction [2].
- **The 98% coverage threshold.** The 98% figure often used to operationalise "comprehensible" text comes from a single regression with 66 New Zealand students. A registered-report replication with 104 Sri Lankan adults found "a mostly linear relationship between vocabulary coverage and reading comprehension" but "failed to replicate an inferred 98% coverage threshold" [7].
- **Still debated.** Lichtman & VanPatten (2021) revisited the question in *Foreign Language Annals* ("Was Krashen right? Forty years later") [6]. Only the record was opened, so its argument is not characterised here.

### Strength of evidence
- **Framework/theory** for the input hypothesis itself.
- **Moderate** that input matters. This rests indirectly on one extensive reading meta-analysis [9], which by this report's own labels is "moderate", not "strong".
- **Strong** that input alone is *not sufficient*. Instruction and feedback add measurable value [11][12][14][15].

### What it means for Lucid
- Large amounts of meaning-focused input (reading, listening, stories) should be a **core strand**, not an add-on.
- It should be combined with explicit focus on form, pushed output and feedback.
- Treat known-word coverage as a **continuous dial** tied to the learner's vocabulary model, not a hard 98% gate [7].

## 3. Output and interaction hypotheses

### Evidence summary
- **Functions of output.** As refined in Swain (1993, 1995), output has four functions, as reported by Izumi [3]:
  - a **fluency** function: developing automaticity;
  - a **noticing/triggering** function: producing language makes learners aware of gaps;
  - **hypothesis testing**;
  - a **metalinguistic (reflective)** function.
- **Mechanism.** Izumi locates output's benefit in "grammatical encoding and monitoring" during production. He notes that situational, linguistic and task variables condition the effect [3]. This is a theoretical review.
- **Interaction.** A *Language Teaching* state-of-the-art review covered interactionist research on input, negotiation for meaning, output, interlocutor characteristics, tasks and computer-mediated interaction [4]. It concludes that interaction generally supports L2 development, but that effectiveness depends on many factors [4].
- **Mackey & Goo (2007).** Their meta-analysis of interaction research appears in an OUP volume [5]. Only the record was opened, so its effect sizes are not reported.
- **Feedback during interaction.**
  - In classroom studies, prompts (learner-repair moves) outperformed recasts. Effects were most apparent on free constructed-response measures [15].
  - Across lab and classroom studies, lab effects were larger [14]. Expect smaller effects in real use.

### Strength of evidence
- **Moderate.** A peer-reviewed state-of-the-art review [4], plus feedback meta-analyses [14][15].
- The output functions are **theory** supported by experimental work that was not opened here.

### What it means for Lucid
- Every unit needs **pushed output**: the learner must produce the target language, not just select options.
- Roleplays should **force negotiation of meaning** (information gaps, clarification requests), not just turn-taking.
- Feedback inside interaction should prefer **prompts** for targeted forms (see §8).

## 4. Task-based (TBLT) and communicative language teaching (CLT)

### Evidence summary
- **CLT as an umbrella.** CLT is now "an umbrella term" for approaches that develop communicative competence in personally meaningful ways [51]. Its universality has been questioned, especially through experience in Asian educational contexts and from a post-method perspective [51].
- **CEFR's action-oriented approach.**
  - It sees learners as "social agents" and directs learning towards acting in real-life situations [1, p. 29].
  - It plans "backwards from learners' real-life communicative needs", aligning curriculum, teaching and assessment [1, p. 29].
  - It is explicitly neutral on method: "There is, for example, no suggestion that one should stop teaching grammar" [1, p. 29].
- **TBLT meta-analysis.** Bryfonski & McKay found "an overall positive and strong effect (d = 0.93)" of TBLT programme implementation across 52 studies, plus positive stakeholder perceptions [52].
- **Critiques of that meta-analysis.**
  - Boers, Bryfonski, Faez & McKay show (Bryfonski and McKay wrote the original meta-analysis) that meta-analytic outcomes depend on how effect sizes are computed, how variables are defined and which studies are included. Their replications of three L2 meta-analyses produced different outcomes [53].
  - Boers & Faez re-examined the 27 comparative studies behind the TBLT effect. With one exception, they "did not serve the intended purpose" [54]. A later re-analysis treated 16 of the 27 as *task-supported* programmes and found a smaller but still positive effect [54]. Boers & Faez nonetheless conclude the field "is not ripe yet" for a meaningful meta-analysis of task-based or task-supported programmes, because "task" is defined inconsistently [54].

### Strength of evidence
- **Limited to moderate** for TBLT's *superiority* over other communicative approaches. The headline d = 0.93 is contested [53][54].
- **Framework** support for task- and action-oriented design [1].

### What it means for Lucid
- Use **task-supported** design: real-world tasks with a non-linguistic outcome, such as booking a room, complaining about a charge, or arguing a position.
- Embed explicit focus on form and practice around those tasks.
- Do not market Lucid as "proven TBLT"; the evidence does not settle that.
- Evaluate tasks by **task success plus language quality**.

## 5. Explicit vs implicit instruction (Norris & Ortega 2000; Spada & Tomita 2010)

### Evidence summary
- **Norris & Ortega (2000).** A meta-analysis of experimental and quasi-experimental studies from 1980 to 1998, with 49 unique samples [11]. It found that:
  - focused L2 instruction produces large gains on targeted features;
  - **explicit instruction outperforms implicit**;
  - Focus on Form and Focus on Forms showed similar, large effects;
  - effects were **durable**;
  - the type of outcome measure influenced effect size.

  The authors caution that generalisability is limited by weak operationalisation and little replication [11].
- **Spada & Tomita (2010).** 41 studies, with grammatical features classified as simple or complex [12]. Explicit instruction yielded larger effects than implicit for **both simple and complex features**. It contributed to "controlled knowledge and spontaneous use" [12].
- **Caveats.** Measurement matters: effects depend on outcome type [11]. Lab effects exceed classroom effects in related feedback research [14]. Much of this evidence uses discrete grammar targets over short treatments.

### Strength of evidence
- **Strong** for direction: two independent meta-analyses agree, and feedback meta-analyses are consistent with them.
- **Moderate** for magnitude in real-world, long-term, spontaneous use.

### What it means for Lucid
- Each grammar point gets a **short explicit explanation**, available in Hebrew at A levels. This is followed by meaning-focused practice and a free-production check.
- Learning is measured with **free production**, not only gap-fills, because measure type inflates effects [11].

## 6. Extensive reading and extensive listening

### Evidence summary
**Jeon & Day (2016)**, meta-analysis of extensive reading (ER) [9]:
- 71 unique samples from 49 studies (1980–2014), N = 5,919.
- **Overall effect:** d = 0.57 [95% CI 0.46–0.68] across 51 experimental-vs-control contrasts; d = 0.79 across 20 pre–post contrasts [9].
- **Moderators** [9]:
  - **Age:** adults d = 0.70; adolescents 0.35; children 0.52.
  - **Setting:** EFL 0.65 vs ESL 0.38.
  - **Medium:** web-based texts 0.89 vs paper 0.47. The authors warn this is based on only k = 10, mostly adult, samples.
  - **Integration:** ER as **part of the curriculum** 0.91 vs ER as part of a reading course 0.47. The authors ask for caution here: most studies fell into these two types, and the other two types had one study each [9]. ER also worked better when combined with other classroom activities than when the whole class was given to reading [9].
  - **Programme length:** effects were similar for short (0.51), medium (0.59) and long (0.60) programmes.
- **Limitations:** most studies were from Asia-Pacific contexts, and long-term studies were rare [9].

**Vocabulary for unassisted comprehension.** Assuming 98% coverage, Nation estimates that a vocabulary of "8,000 to 9,000 word-family" is needed for written text and "6,000 to 7,000 for spoken text" [8]. But the 98% threshold did not replicate, and the coverage–comprehension relation looks roughly linear and genre-dependent [7].

**Extensive listening.** No meta-analysis was opened.
- One quasi-experimental study compared three groups (N = 114 Japanese EFL learners): no text, English captions, and Japanese subtitles [10]. Subtitles changed strategy use (more imagery and summarisation). It measured strategies, not learning gains [10].

### Strength of evidence
- **Strong to moderate** for ER: a meta-analysis with mostly positive, heterogeneous effects, concentrated in Asia-Pacific.
- **Limited** for extensive listening and for caption policy.

### What it means for Lucid
- ER should be **built into the curriculum**, with weekly quotas counted toward progress. It should not be an optional library.
- The 16+ adult audience is where the ER effect was largest [9].
- Texts are chosen by **estimated known-word coverage** from the learner's vocabulary model. Coverage is a tunable parameter (§ Implementation decisions), not a fixed rule.
- Hebrew-subtitle vs English-caption defaults are a **hypothesis to A/B test**, not an evidence-based rule.

## 7. Pronunciation: intelligibility vs nativeness; computer-assisted pronunciation training (CAPT)

### Evidence summary
- **Lee, Jang & Plonsky (2015).** Meta-analysis of 86 reports of pronunciation instruction [48]:
  - d = 0.89 for within-group contrasts and d = 0.80 for between-group contrasts.
  - Larger effects with **longer interventions**, **treatments that included feedback** and **more controlled outcome measures** [48]. Gains on controlled tasks may overstate spontaneous-speech gains.
- **Munro & Derwing (1995).** This *Language Learning* study treated foreign accent, comprehensibility and intelligibility as separate dimensions of L2 speech [49]. A conceptual replication with L2 Spanish learners (Huensch & Nagle, 2021) is recorded as **successful**: results patterned similarly, particularly "the robustness of partial independence among speech dimensions" [49]. Only the replication record was opened; the original was not.
- **CEFR.** The CV rebuilt its phonology scale around **intelligibility** and calls the traditional accent focus "detrimental" [1, p. 133].
- **ASR-based training.** Ngo, Chen & Lai (2024) meta-analysed 15 studies with 38 effect sizes (2008–2021) [50]:
  - **Overall:** medium effect, g = 0.69.
  - **Feedback type:** ASR with **explicit corrective feedback** g = 0.86 vs indirect feedback such as ASR dictation g = 0.50.
  - **Target:** **segmental** g = 0.82 vs **suprasegmental** g = 0.37.
  - **Duration:** short treatments of 1–4 weeks g = 0.07 (no differential effect; only 3 effect sizes from 3 studies); medium, 5–8 weeks, g = 1.01 (7 effect sizes, 4 studies); long, 9 weeks or more, g = 0.72 (28 effect sizes, 8 studies).
  - **Social setting:** practising **with peers** g = 0.89 vs **alone** 0.44.
  - **Learners:** largely effective for adults and intermediate learners.
  - **Limitations:** few primary studies and no publication-bias test [50].
  - **Contrast with an older CAPT meta-analysis.** Ngo et al. report that Mahdi & Al Khateeb (2019) found the opposite pattern (larger suprasegmental effects), but that review mixed in multimedia rather than ASR [50] (secondary report).
  - **Example primary study.** One study cited in their introduction (Evers & Chen, 2022) found that an ASR dictation tool improved comprehensibility and spontaneous speech but **not accentedness** [50] (secondary report).

### Strength of evidence
- **Moderate to strong** that instruction and feedback improve pronunciation [48][50].
- **Moderate** for ASR specifics (few studies).
- **Framework** support for an intelligibility target [1].
- **No Hebrew-L1-specific evidence** was opened.

### What it means for Lucid
- Score **intelligibility/comprehensibility, never "accent"**.
- ASR exercises must give **explicit, localised feedback** (which sound and where), not just a transcript.
- Programmes must run **beyond four weeks**.
- Add **partner/peer** practice modes.
- Supplement ASR with perception and prosody work, because ASR helps prosody least.

## 8. Oral corrective feedback (Lyster & Ranta 1997; Li 2010)

### Evidence summary
- **Lyster & Ranta (1997).** 18.3 hours of interaction in four primary French-immersion classrooms [13]. They identified six feedback types.
  - The abstract reports "an overwhelming tendency" for teachers to use **recasts**, which were ineffective at eliciting student-generated repair.
  - **Elicitation, metalinguistic feedback, clarification requests and repetition** led to repair more often and initiated "negotiation of form" [13].
  - This is an observational study: uptake/repair is not the same as acquisition.
- **Li (2010).** Meta-analysis of 33 studies (22 published, 11 dissertations) [14]:
  - **Overall:** a **medium overall effect** of CF, maintained over time.
  - **Implicit vs explicit:** implicit feedback was better maintained than explicit.
  - **Setting:** lab-based studies showed larger effects than classroom studies, and foreign-language contexts larger effects than second-language contexts.
  - **Duration:** shorter treatments produced larger effects.
  - **Publication bias:** published studies did not show larger effects than dissertations [14].
- **Lyster & Saito (2010).** 15 classroom studies (N = 827) [15]:
  - CF had **significant and durable** effects.
  - Effects were **larger for prompts than recasts**. CF effects overall were "most apparent in measures that elicit free constructed responses" [15]. The abstract does not tie that measure effect to prompts specifically.
  - Long treatments outperformed short-to-medium ones.
  - Younger learners benefited more [15]. Lucid's learners are 16+, so expect somewhat smaller effects.

### Strength of evidence
**Strong** that oral CF helps (two meta-analyses plus an observational base). **Moderate** on which type is best: prompts win in classrooms [15], while implicit feedback is better retained across lab and classroom [14].

### What it means for Lucid
- In roleplays and with the AI tutor, use **prompts** (elicitation, metalinguistic clue, clarification request) for the lesson's target forms.
- Use brief recasts for non-target errors that impede meaning.
- Keep corrections few per turn, and let the learner control intensity.
- Report **retention in later, unprompted use** as the success metric, not immediate repair.

## 9. Written corrective feedback (WCF): the Truscott debate; focused vs unfocused

### Evidence summary
**The case against.**
- Truscott (1996) argued grammar correction "should be abandoned", for three reasons: research shows it ineffective; theory predicts it to be ineffective; and "it has harmful effects" [16].
- He extended the argument in a 2007 *Journal of Second Language Writing* paper [17]. Only its bibliographic record was opened, so its estimate is not reported here.

**Meta-analytic evidence for WCF.**
- **Kang & Han (2015)** synthesised 21 studies: "written corrective feedback can lead to greater grammatical accuracy in second language writing" [18]. Efficacy depended on **learner proficiency**, the learning setting and the writing genre [18].
- **Lim & Renandya (2020)** synthesised 35 studies, after removing 3 of 38 as outliers [19]:
  - a moderate overall effect, g = 0.59 [95% CI 0.42–0.76];
  - direct feedback g = 0.76 vs indirect g = 0.63, a difference that was **not significant**;
  - learner proficiency was the strongest moderator ("mitigator") of the ten examined.
- **Reynolds & Kao (2022)** synthesised 34 studies of **unfocused** (comprehensive) feedback [20]. It was effective on immediate posttests, with benefits that appeared to grow on delayed tests. But only 12 studies reported delayed tests [20].

**Key experiments.**
- **van Beuningen, de Jong & Kuiken (2012).** N = 268 L2 learners [21].
  - Both **direct** and **indirect** comprehensive feedback improved accuracy over self-editing and extra-practice controls, including in **new texts**.
  - Only direct feedback improved **grammatical** accuracy in new writing. Indirect feedback helped **non-grammatical** accuracy most.
  - Feedback did **not** make learners simplify their writing [21]. This directly tests two of Truscott's claims.
- **Bitchener & Knoch.** A ten-month focused-WCF study of 52 ESL learners targeted "only two functional error categories" [22]. Its *Applied Linguistics* report (2010) has a conceptual replication (Ekiert & di Gennaro, 2019) that only "partially confirm[s]" the results [23].

**Focused vs unfocused.**
- Ellis, Sheen, Murakami & Takashima (2008) directly compared focused and unfocused WCF in an EFL context [24]. Its findings could not be opened.
- A 2024 review notes that many researchers favour direct and focused WCF. It also notes counter-arguments that all types show some effect, and that learner proficiency and beliefs strongly shape outcomes [25].

### Strength of evidence
- **Strong to moderate** that WCF improves accuracy, including in new writing [18][19][21].
- **Contested and moderate** on focused vs unfocused, and on direct vs indirect: differences are often non-significant [19], and proficiency moderates them [18][19].
- Truscott's critique stands as a methodological caution. Revision gains are not learning, so delayed new-text measures are needed.

### What it means for Lucid
- Give feedback, but **prioritise**: show a small number of error categories per draft, keyed to the learner's level.
- Use **direct** correction for grammar, and **indirect** cues (location plus category) for non-grammatical errors [21].
- Moving grammar feedback to indirect cues at higher levels is a **design hypothesis to A/B test**, not an evidence-based rule. In [21], only direct CF improved grammatical accuracy in new texts. Proficiency moderates WCF effects [18][19], but the opened sources do not say which feedback type suits which level.
- Measure success as **accuracy on the next new text**, not on the revised draft.

## 10. Automated writing evaluation (AWE)

### Evidence summary
- **Fleckenstein, Liebenow & Meyer (2023).** Multi-level meta-analysis of 20 studies (k = 84 effects, N = 2,828) [26]. It found a **medium effect, g = 0.55**, of automated feedback on writing performance. Heterogeneity was significant, "automated feedback tools cannot be understood as a single consistent form of intervention", and no moderator comparison reached significance [26].
- **Stevenson & Phakiti (2014).** This critical review is summarised in its Semantic Scholar record as finding **modest evidence** that AWE improves the quality of texts written with it, and little evidence of transfer to general writing proficiency [27]. That summary is machine-generated; the full text was not opened. A 2023 study similarly describes AWE results on holistic scores and accuracy as "inconclusive" [28].
- **Accuracy and uptake problems**, as reported by Shen et al. (2023) [28] (secondary):
  - **Accuracy by error type:** Bai & Hu (2017) found one widely used Chinese AWE tool (Pigai) correct **98%** of the time for mechanics, **58%** for grammar and **22%** for collocations.
  - **Uptake** tracked accuracy: 74%, 51% and 12% respectively.
  - **Transfer:** Choi (2010) found within-draft gains that did not transfer to a later test.
- Shen et al.'s own study (42 learners, 5 essays over 14 weeks) observed [28]:
  - feedback that stayed largely **lexical/mechanical**;
  - learner reports of **inaccurate flags**: for example, a correct sentence flagged as a fragment, and wider distrust of the tool's precision (qualitative reports, not a measured error rate);
  - low uptake of non-corrective suggestions.

  It had no control group.

### Strength of evidence
- **Moderate** for short-term text-quality gains [26].
- **Limited** for transfer to unassisted writing [27][28].
- Tool accuracy varies sharply by error type [28].

### What it means for Lucid
- AI writing evaluation is a **formative** tool: drafts, revision and targeted practice.
- Its precision must be **measured per error type** and published internally.
- Low-precision categories (e.g. collocation) should be shown as suggestions, or suppressed.

## 11. AI chatbots and LLM tutors in language learning (2023–2026): benefits and risks

### Evidence summary
**Benefits.**
- **Huang, Hew & Fryer (2022)** systematically reviewed 25 chatbot studies [29].
  - **Affordances:** timeliness, ease of use and personalisation.
  - **Uses:** chatbots as interlocutors, simulations, a means of transmission, helplines and recommenders.
  - **Challenges:** "technological limitations, the novelty effect, and cognitive load" [29].
- **Wang, Cheung, Neitzel & Chai (2024)** meta-analysed 70 effect sizes from 28 studies [30]. Chatbots showed a **positive overall effect, g = 0.484**, versus non-chatbot conditions. The effect was moderated by educational level, language level, **interface design** and **interaction capability** [30].

**Feedback quality of an LLM on student writing.**
- **Steiss et al. (2024)** compared 200 human and 200 ChatGPT feedback texts on the same secondary-school essays (grades 6–12) [31].
  - The essays were source-based argument essays in history, from Southern California classrooms.
  - The sample was 50 English learners, 50 reclassified-fluent students and 100 fluent English speakers. It was not adult L2 learners.
- Humans were better on all dimensions except **criteria alignment**, where the AI scored higher (d = −0.22). The human advantage was largest for prioritising essential features (d = 0.82) and accuracy (d = 0.61).
- Accuracy: humans 4.55 vs AI 4.0, on a 1–5 scale.
- **AI accuracy fell as essay quality rose**: 4.42, 4.05 and 3.31 for low-, average- and high-quality essays.
- Feedback quality did not differ by English-learner status [31].

**"Hallucinated" or over-eager corrections.**
- **Fang et al. (2023):** ChatGPT's grammatical error correction (GEC) output is highly fluent "possibly due to its over-correction tendencies and not adhering to the principle of minimal edits". At document level it failed on cross-sentence agreement, coreference and tense errors [32].
- **Wu et al. (2023):** ChatGPT under-performed Grammarly and the GECToR model on the CoNLL-2014 automatic metric. In human evaluation it made fewer under-corrections and mis-corrections but **more over-corrections**, often rewriting structure rather than making isolated fixes [33].
- Both are early-model (GPT-3.5-era) preprints. Current models may differ, which is why Lucid must measure its own.

**Over-reliance.**
- **Bastani et al. (2025)** ran a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high-school maths students [34]:
  - GPT-4 access raised practice grades: by 48% with a standard chat interface ("GPT Base") and by 127% with a tutor version prompted to protect learning ("GPT Tutor").
  - When access was removed, **GPT Base students scored 17% lower than students who never had access**. GPT Tutor's safeguards "largely" mitigated the harm [34].
  - This is maths, not L2, but it is the strongest causal evidence on the guardrail question.
- **Kosmyna et al. (2025)**, an MIT Media Lab preprint (54 participants; 18 in session 4), compared essay writing with an LLM, a search engine or no tool [35]:
  - The LLM group showed the weakest EEG connectivity and the lowest self-reported ownership of their essays.
  - LLM users "struggled to accurately quote their own work".
  - It is an arXiv preprint (v2, December 2025). No peer-reviewed version was checked in this pass, and the sample is small [35].

### Strength of evidence
- **Moderate** for chatbot learning gains: meta-analysis g ≈ 0.48, with moderators [30]. The novelty effect is a documented challenge [29]. The typical length of the primary studies was not checked.
- **Moderate** for LLM feedback quality being somewhat below trained teachers and worse on strong essays [31].
- **Limited to moderate** on over-reliance: one strong RCT outside L2 [34] and one preprint [35].
- **Limited, but consistent**, on over-correction [32][33].

### What it means for Lucid
- The AI tutor must be designed **as a tutor, not an answer engine**:
  - **attempt-first**: no model answer before the learner's attempt;
  - **hints before solutions**;
  - **minimal-edit** corrections;
  - **unassisted checkpoints** to measure real learning.
- In [31], AI feedback accuracy fell as essay quality rose. Applying this to Lucid's C1–C2 L2 argumentative writing is an **extrapolation**: [31] studied grade 6–12 history essays, not adult L2 writing at C levels. Treat the C-level track as the **likely** weakest area, and measure it before relying on it. Uncertainty flags and optional human review belong there.

## 12. Evidence on app-based learning efficacy (including who funded it)

### Evidence summary
- **Vesselinov & Grego (2012).** **Commissioned and funded by Duolingo**, with the researchers reported to have collected and analysed data independently [36]. The headline is 34 hours of Duolingo use to cover one college semester of Spanish (WebCAPE placement test, about 8 points gained per hour).
  - Many participants dropped out or studied very little [36].
  - Gains differed by motive: 17.6 points/hour for travel-motivated learners vs 5.7 for those studying for personal interest or school [36].
  - The original report was not opened; these details come from contemporary press coverage.
- **Broderick (2020).** An independent test with Japanese university students on the TOEIC Bridge (N = 116 after screening) [37]:
  - Each hour of Duolingo predicted **0.121 points** of score gain (R² = .042, a small effect).
  - The author notes that compulsory-course students are less motivated than Vesselinov & Grego's volunteer adults, and that the correlation may reflect motivation, not causation [37].
- **Jiang, Rollinson, Plonsky, Gustafson & Pajak (2021).** *Foreign Language Annals*, 225 US adults who completed beginner Spanish or French courses with Duolingo as their only tool [38]:
  - They reached **Intermediate Low** (reading) and **Novice High** (listening) on ACTFL tests, comparable to fourth-semester university students.
  - **Only reading and listening were assessed** [38].
- **Taylor (2022)** reviewed Duolingo's in-house reports [39]:
  - **Authorship:** the researchers "were almost entirely Duolingo employees".
  - **Selection bias:**
    - Of 8,367 completers contacted, 813 responded, 499 were eligible and 195 started the test.
    - A certificate incentive likely attracted stronger learners.
    - 90% of one Duolingo sample held at least a bachelor's degree.
  - **Comparability:** the comparison groups were mostly compulsory-requirement undergraduates [39]. Taylor notes that the authors acknowledged the comparability problem themselves [39].
- **Loewen et al. (2019).** An independent semester-long case study of 9 learners of Turkish [40]:
  - **Gains:** learners improved, with a moderate positive correlation between time on the app and gains.
  - **Motivation:** it was variable, and learners were frustrated by the instructional materials [40].
  - The article contrasts one commissioned study with favourable outcomes against independent research raising concerns about persistence, motivation and efficacy [40].
- **Shortt et al. (2023; online 2021).** A systematic review of 35 Duolingo studies (from 367 records) [41]. Most were design-focused and quantitative, with **non-probability sampling** [41].
- **Placement-test research is also vendor research.** The Duolingo English Test's validation paper is authored by Duolingo staff ("Research conducted at Duolingo") [42].

### Strength of evidence
**Limited.** Vendor-funded or vendor-authored studies dominate. They use self-selected completers, have high attrition and lack randomised controls. Independent studies are small or show small effects [37][40].

### What it means for Lucid
- Lucid's efficacy claims must:
  - come from studies with **pre-registered outcomes**;
  - use **intention-to-treat** reporting, so dropouts count;
  - disclose **who funded and who analysed**;
  - test **all four skills plus writing**.
- No "X hours = one semester" marketing.

## 13. Gamification and motivation (self-determination theory)

### Evidence summary
- **Self-determination theory (SDT).** Three innate psychological needs are proposed: competence, autonomy and relatedness. When satisfied they "yield enhanced self-motivation and mental health". When thwarted they "lead to diminished motivation and well-being" [43].
- **Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999).** Meta-analysis of 128 experiments [45]:
  - **Expected tangible rewards undermined** free-choice intrinsic motivation. By reward type: engagement-contingent d = −0.40, completion-contingent d = −0.36, performance-contingent d = −0.28.
  - Engagement- and completion-contingent rewards also lowered self-reported interest (d = −0.15 and −0.17).
  - **Positive feedback increased** free-choice behaviour (d = 0.33) and interest (d = 0.31) [45].
- **Sailer & Homner (2020).** Meta-analysis of gamification in learning [46]:
  - **Effect sizes:** small positive effects on cognitive (g = 0.49, k = 19), motivational (g = 0.36, k = 16) and behavioural (g = 0.25, k = 9) outcomes.
  - **Robustness:** only the cognitive effect held in high-rigour studies; motivational and behavioural effects were "less stable".
  - **What helped:** game fiction, and **competition combined with collaboration**, were particularly effective for behavioural outcomes [46].
- **Hanus & Fox (2015).** A longitudinal classroom study [47]. According to the Semantic Scholar record's machine-generated summary, students in a **gamified course** showed less motivation, satisfaction and empowerment over time [47]. The summary also urges care with certain gamification mechanics [47].
  - The specific gamification mechanics and the study's length could not be checked in the opened records. An earlier draft named "badges, leaderboard" and called it "semester-long"; both details are now marked (unverified).
  - The full abstract was not opened, so this is a weak citation.
- **Gamification in Duolingo research.** The review in [41] assessed Duolingo's challenges, rewards, levels and rankings, but found the evidence base design-focused and non-probabilistic [41]. In the case study, motivation was variable despite positive views of gamification [40].

### Strength of evidence
- **Moderate to strong** that expected, contingent tangible rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation, and that informational positive feedback helps [45]. The evidence is one large meta-analysis of 128 experiments in general (not L2) motivation research. By this report's labels, a single meta-analysis is "moderate".
- **Moderate** that gamification gives small learning gains [46].
- **Limited** on specific harms of leaderboards and streaks [47].

### What it means for Lucid
- Motivate through **competence** (visible can-do progress), **autonomy** (choice of topics, tasks and goals) and **relatedness** (collaborative challenges).
- Use informational feedback rather than completion-contingent loot.
- Public competition should be opt-in.

## 14. Placement testing and adaptive testing basics (CAT, item difficulty)

### Evidence summary
- **The Rasch model.** Most modern high-stakes tests rest on item response theory (IRT). The Rasch model gives the probability of a correct response as p = 1 / (1 + exp(δ − θ)), where δ is item difficulty and θ is ability [42].
- **The CAT loop.** Estimate θ by maximum likelihood from responses so far. Select the next item's difficulty from that estimate. Repeat "until reaching some stopping criterion" [42]. Adaptivity also improves security, but only if the item bank is large enough to keep exposure low [42].
- **Machine-learned difficulty.** Settles, LaFlair & Hagiwara estimated item difficulty with ML/NLP models trained on CEFR-labelled data, instead of pilot testing [42]:
  - **Score scale:** CEFR-mapped, A1 = 0, A2 = 20, B1 = 40, B2 = 60, C1 = 80, C2 = 100.
  - **Agreement with pilot-based IRT:** a post-hoc Rasch fit on 524,921 responses gave **Spearman ρ = .96** between the ML-based scores and conventional IRT θ.
  - **Reliability:** internal consistency .96 (n = 9,309); test–retest .80 (n = 526).
  - **Concurrent validity, CAT only:** the CAT-only scores correlated r > .7 with TOEFL iBT (n = 2,319) and IELTS (n = 991) [42].
  - **Updated test:** the figures of test–retest .85, TOEFL .77 and IELTS .78 apply to the later test that **adds automatically graded writing and speaking sections**, not to the receptive CAT alone [42]. The authors suggest the receptive-item CAT may overestimate overall ability relative to tests with open-ended production [42].
  - **Caveat:** this is vendor-authored. Settles and LaFlair are at Duolingo; Hagiwara is at Octanove Labs, with the research "conducted at Duolingo". The external scores were self-reported. The authors also note the CEFR is "descriptive (not prescriptive)", giving little guidance on specific items [42].
- **Developing a CAT.** Thompson & Weiss (2011) combine 40 years of technical research into a practical development framework covering item selection algorithms, exposure control and termination criteria [44]. They note there is "little literature providing practical guidance on the development of a CAT" [44].
- **What the CEFR says about levels and tests.** A test can be *related* to the CEFR only through a documented linking procedure. Descriptors are not test items, and results should be read as profiles [1, pp. 29, 38, 41].

### Strength of evidence
**Moderate** for CAT/IRT as psychometric practice. The validation evidence cited is vendor-authored [42]. The framework source [44] was read at abstract level.

### What it means for Lucid
- Placement is an **adaptive, Rasch-calibrated receptive test** plus a short productive sample.
- It outputs an **assigned starting level with uncertainty**.
- The level is **re-estimated** from lesson performance, and **assessed** at checkpoints with criterion-referenced tasks.

## Implementation decisions for Lucid

Each decision has a default, a testable acceptance criterion, and the evidence it rests on. Numeric parameters marked *(tunable)* are design starting points to validate with Lucid's own data. They are not values taken from the literature.

### A. Levels, CEFR alignment and assessment
1. **Level model.** Use Pre-A1, A1, A2, A2+, B1, B1+, B2, B2+, C1, C2: the criterion levels plus the CV's plus levels [1, pp. 38, 40].
   - *Correction (verification pass):* an earlier draft also listed "A1+". The CV's descriptor scales and sample profiles have no A1+ band [1, p. 40]. If Lucid wants an A1+ step, label it a Lucid-internal sub-band, not a CEFR level.
   - Store a **per-activity profile**: reading, listening, spoken production, written production, spoken interaction, written/online interaction, mediation. This mirrors the CV's sample profile [1, p. 40].
   - Never store only one global level [1, p. 38].
   - *Test:* the progress screen shows at least four activity bars, with a "plus" state.
2. **Two kinds of level.** Separate the **assigned level**, used for course placement, from the **assessed level**, which is evidence-based.
   - The assessed level updates only from criterion-referenced checkpoint tasks scored against competence criteria [1, pp. 29, 41, 43].
   - UI copy says "Lucid estimate aligned to CEFR descriptors", **never** "certified B2" [1, p. 29].
   - *Test:* no string in the app asserts certification. Checkpoint scores carry a confidence band.
3. **Can-do checklists.** Self-assessment lists are capped at **10–20 descriptors per module**. Activity ("can do") descriptors are used for self-assessment; competence descriptors are used for scoring grids with **4–5 criteria** [1, p. 43].
   - *Test:* the content linter rejects modules with more than 20 self-assessment descriptors or grids with more than 5 criteria.
4. **Pre-A1 support.** Pre-A1/A1 production tasks allow **menu selection, templates, an in-app dictionary and Hebrew glosses**, mirroring the descriptors [1, pp. 66, 82, 86]. Use of these supports is logged and reported, and is not penalised.
5. **Mediation and plurilingual strand.** From A2, every second unit includes a mediation task, rising to every unit at B2+ *(tunable)*. Examples:
   - Hebrew text → English summary for a visitor;
   - English email → Hebrew explanation for a family member.

   Tasks are tagged to the CV mediation and plurilingual scales [1, pp. 90, 127].
6. **Writing ladder.** Writing prompts and rubrics are anchored to "Overall written production", "Reports and essays" and "Written interaction" [1, pp. 66, 68, 82]:
   - B1: opinion texts with pros and cons;
   - B2: argument with reasons for and against, and synthesis of at least 2 sources;
   - C1: multi-paragraph exposition with required redrafting;
   - C2: multiple perspectives, with own voice distinguished from sources.
   - *Test:* each writing task declares its descriptor IDs.

### B. Input, output and instruction design
7. **Input strand.** A curriculum-integrated extensive reading quota of **≥ 60 minutes/week** *(tunable)* counts toward unit completion. Texts are learner-chosen within a level-filtered library, because ER integrated into the curriculum showed the largest effect [9].
   - *Metric:* words read per week, and pre/post reading-rate and comprehension checks each term.
8. **Text difficulty.** Recommend extensive (fluency) texts at **≥ 95% estimated known-word coverage, target 98%**, and intensive texts at **90–95% with glosses** *(tunable)*. Coverage is a dial that ranks texts, not a pass/fail gate, because the 98% threshold did not replicate [7][8].
   - *A/B test:* compare 95% vs 98% targets on completion rate and comprehension.
9. **Captions.** Default to **English captions** on video from A2. Offer Hebrew subtitles as a toggle at Pre-A1–A1. This is a hypothesis under A/B test [10], not an evidence-based rule.
10. **Explicit + practice cycle per grammar point:**
    1. ≤ 90-second explicit explanation, in Hebrew available to B1;
    2. controlled practice;
    3. meaning-focused task;
    4. free-production check.

    This follows the meta-analyses favouring explicit instruction [11][12]. Mastery requires the **free-production** check, not only controlled items [11].
11. **Pushed output.** Every lesson ends with ≥ 1 spoken or written production task that requires the lesson's targets [3][4]. The lesson cannot be marked complete with selection-only items.
12. **Task-supported roleplays.** Each simulation defines:
    - a **non-linguistic goal** (e.g. get a refund);
    - an **information gap**;
    - success criteria for both task outcome and language [1, p. 29][51][52].

    Report task success and language quality separately. Do not claim "TBLT is proven superior" [53][54].

### C. Feedback policies
13. **Oral and roleplay corrective feedback defaults** [13][14][15]:
    - For **lesson-target forms**, use **prompts**: elicitation, a metalinguistic clue or a clarification request.
    - For non-target errors that block meaning, use a **brief recast**.
    - Make **≤ 1 correction per learner turn** in fluency mode. Give a delayed **post-task summary** of the remaining errors (max 3) *(tunable)*.
    - Learner setting: Correction intensity = Off / Targets only (default) / All.
    - *Metric:* accuracy on the same forms in **later, unprompted** turns (retention), not immediate repair.
14. **Written feedback defaults** [18][19][20][21]:
    - **Focused by default:** show **≤ 3 error categories per draft**, prioritised by the learner's level and current targets. Offer "Show all errors" on demand, since comprehensive feedback also has support [20][21].
      - The ≤ 3-category cap is a design choice *(tunable)*. The study cited here that directly compares focused and unfocused WCF [24] was read only as a bibliographic record, so no source here shows focused beats comprehensive.
    - **Default:** **direct** correction for grammar, and indirect cues (location + category code) for non-grammatical errors, at all levels [21].
    - **Experimental arm, not a default:** indirect feedback for grammar at B2+.
      - *Evidence weakened (verification pass):* in [21], only direct CF improved grammatical accuracy in new texts. The opened meta-analyses show proficiency moderates WCF [18][19], but not that indirect feedback suits higher levels. Direct and indirect did not differ significantly in [19].
      - Ship the B2+ indirect-for-grammar rule only if an A/B test shows it is no worse on next-text accuracy.
    - A **revision step is required**.
    - Success is measured on **accuracy in the next new text**, compared with the learner's own baseline over a delayed interval of 2+ weeks *(tunable)* [21]. [21] used new texts 1 and 4 weeks after feedback.
      - *Correction:* an earlier draft also cited [17], but only that paper's bibliographic record was opened, so it cannot support this decision.

### D. AI evaluation, tutor and guardrails
15. **Minimal-edit correction engine.**
    - The LLM corrector is constrained to **minimal edits**. Output is a diff, with every edit tagged by category and a one-line rule.
    - "Fluency rewrite" suggestions are a **separate, labelled layer** and never mixed with corrections [32][33].
16. **Precision gates per error type.**
    - Maintain a gold set of **≥ 300 annotated Hebrew-L1 learner sentences per CEFR band** *(tunable)*.
    - Ship an error category as a "correction" only if **precision ≥ 95%**. Categories at **80–95%** are shown as "suggestion". Categories **< 80%** are suppressed *(tunable thresholds)*. Collocation is expected to start in the lower tiers [28].
    - Re-run on every model or prompt change.
17. **Second-check on corrections.** Every correction passes a verification step: a second model pass or a rule-based checker. Disagreements are shown as **"uncertain — check"** rather than as a correction [32][33].
18. **High-level writing safeguards.**
    - For C1–C2 essays, feedback leads on **argument structure, source use and cohesion** against the CEFR "Reports and essays" descriptors [1, p. 68].
    - *Evidence weakened:* the claim that LLM accuracy is lowest here is extrapolated. [31] found AI accuracy fell on higher-quality grade 6–12 history essays, not on adult L2 C-level writing. Validate it with Lucid's own gold set (decision 16) before relying on it.
    - Each comment shows a confidence label.
    - Offer **optional human review**.
19. **Writing scores are formative.** AI rubric scores appear as ranges with "formative estimate" labels. They never update the *assessed* CEFR level without a calibrated checkpoint [1, p. 41][26][27].
20. **Tutor guardrails (attempt-first)** [34][35]:
    - The tutor gives **no model answer** before the learner submits an attempt.
    - Help ladder: hint → targeted question → partial example → full model.
    - The tutor **never drafts graded writing** for the learner, and the writing lab has no AI autocomplete.
    - *Metric:* AI-assist ratio per task, logged and shown to the learner.
21. **Unassisted checkpoints.** Every unit ends with a **no-AI** checkpoint covering reading, listening and writing. A rising gap between assisted and unassisted performance triggers reduced hints [34].
22. **Chatbot interlocutor design.** Provide **voice and text** modes and level-controlled language, since interface design and interaction capability moderate effects [30]. Evaluate retention at **week 4 or later** *(tunable)* to discount the novelty effect [29]. The novelty effect is documented in [29]; the 4-week cut-off is our design choice and does not come from a source.

### E. Pronunciation
23. **Intelligibility target.** Score **intelligibility/comprehensibility** (e.g. ASR word-recognition rate plus a listener-effort proxy). Never show an "accent" or "native-likeness" score [1, p. 133][49].
24. **ASR practice design** [48][50]:
    - **Explicit, localised feedback:** which phoneme and where.
    - Not dictation-only.
    - Pronunciation modules run **≥ 6 weeks** *(tunable)*, since 1–4 weeks showed no differential effect [50].
      - *Caveat:* that duration contrast rests on very few studies: 3 effect sizes from 3 studies for short treatments, and 4 studies for medium ones [50]. Treat it as a weak default.
    - Offer a **partner/peer practice mode**.
    - Add perception and prosody (stress, intonation) exercises, because ASR helps suprasegmentals least.
    - Hebrew-L1 priority sounds are to be defined by the content team; no evidence was opened here.

### F. Motivation and gamification
25. **SDT-aligned rewards** [43][45][46]:
    - The primary feedback loop is **informational**: can-do progress, mastered forms, words read.
    - **No expected tangible rewards for mere completion or engagement**, such as paywalled loot for streaks.
    - Celebrate competence milestones with specific feedback.
26. **Competition is opt-in.**
    - Leaderboards are **off by default**.
    - Prefer **collaborative or team challenges**, or competition combined with collaboration [46][47].
27. **Streaks are forgiving.**
    - Use weekly goals rather than daily-only targets.
    - Include automatic grace days.
    - No loss-framed shaming copy.

    This is grounded in SDT [43][45]; it is a hypothesis to test, since no streak-specific study was opened.

### G. Placement and progress testing
28. **Placement CAT** [42][44]:
    - **Item model:** Rasch-calibrated item bank for reading, listening, vocabulary and grammar.
    - **Start and stop:** start at A2 difficulty, or from a self-report, with a "no English yet" route straight to Pre-A1. Stop when the ability estimate's **SEM ≤ 0.35 logits or at 30 items** *(tunable; validate by simulation)*.
    - **Productive sample:** 1 short writing sample + 1 spoken sample, AI-scored as a formative estimate.
    - **Output:** an **assigned** starting level with a confidence band.
    - **Recalibration:** item difficulties are first estimated from CEFR-labelled data or expert judgement, then **re-estimated from live responses**, with drift checks [42].
    - *Acceptance test:* simulation shows a classification consistency target, plus test–retest on a pilot cohort.
29. **Early re-placement.** After 5 lessons *(tunable)*, compare lesson accuracy with the expected accuracy for the assigned level, and offer to move up or down. This treats placement as assignment, not assessment [1, p. 41].
30. **Efficacy research protocol.** Before any public efficacy claim:
    - pre-registered outcomes;
    - **intention-to-treat** analysis;
    - an independent analyst or published data;
    - all skills tested;
    - funding disclosed [36][37][38][39][40].

## What not to build

- **No CEFR "certification" or single-letter level from XP or lesson completion.** The CEFR is not a standardisation tool, descriptors are not tests, and linking requires a formal procedure [1, pp. 29, 41].
- **No accent-reduction or "sound native" score.** Intelligibility is the target [1, p. 133][49].
- **No default "red-pen everything" feedback.** This is a **design choice, not an evidence-based prohibition**.
  - Proficiency moderates WCF effects [18][19].
  - The claim that comprehensive feedback "overloads learners" is (unverified): no opened source tests it.
  - Comprehensive (unfocused) feedback was itself effective in [20] and [21].
  - "Show all" stays one tap away, and the focused-vs-comprehensive default should be A/B tested.
- **No unconstrained LLM rewriting presented as "corrections".** Over-correction is documented [32][33].
- **No essay-writing AI or autocomplete in the writing lab, and no answer-first tutor.** Unguarded access harmed later unassisted performance in a maths RCT [34]. A small essay-writing preprint points the same way [35]. No L2-specific RCT was opened.
- **No public leaderboards by default.** No completion-contingent tangible rewards, and no punitive or loss-framed streak mechanics [45][46][47].
- **No input-only course and no grammar-drill-only course.** The evidence supports input plus explicit instruction plus output plus feedback [2][3][9][11][12].
- **No hard 98%-coverage gate on reading** [7].
- **No ASR "pronunciation course" of under ~4 weeks with only dictation feedback** [50].
- **No efficacy marketing built on self-selected completers** (e.g. "N hours = a university semester") [36][37][39].
- **No AI-scored writing that silently changes the assessed CEFR level** [26][27][31].
