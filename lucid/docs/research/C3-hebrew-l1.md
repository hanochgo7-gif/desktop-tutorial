# Team C3 — Hebrew-speaking learners of English: L1 transfer, Israeli context and L1 support

> **Method.** Desk research done on 2026-10-30. Search results were used only to *find* sources. Every citation [n] points to a source that was opened in this session with WebFetch: publisher landing pages, institutional repository records, open-access PDFs, official Israeli Ministry of Education (MoE) and NITE documents, and scholarly databases (PHOIBLE, WALS). Some PDFs came back from WebFetch as binary: the MoE English Curriculum 2020, the 3-point and 5-point Bagrut handbooks, the NITE examinee guide, Zaltz & Segal (2022), Bat-El et al. (2019), Uchihara et al. (2024), Hall & Cook (2013), Levitzky-Aviad (2012) and Immelman & Cooper (2023). WebFetch saved these files, and they were converted to text locally with `pdftotext`, so the full text could be read.
>
> **Limitations, stated up front.**
> - **There is no Hebrew chapter in Swan & Smith's *Learner English*.** The brief assumed one. The publisher's description of the 2nd edition (2001), as reproduced by a bookseller, lists chapters for 22 to 23 L1 groups, from Dutch to Thai, and Hebrew is not among them [9]. A library record for the 1987 1st edition confirmed the editors and publisher but showed no chapter list [10]. The book was therefore **not** used as a Hebrew source. Its Arabic chapter covers another Semitic language, but it was not opened, and Arabic-to-English transfer cannot be assumed to match Hebrew-to-English transfer.
> - **Hebrew-specific empirical evidence is thin.** No peer-reviewed study was found that measures Hebrew-L1 adults' English vowel production, /θ ð/ or /w/ substitution, article omission, copula omission, or present-progressive and present-perfect errors. For these, the report gives **typological predictions** from Hebrew phonology and grammar descriptions [1][3][11]. A prediction is *not* evidence that learners make the error, and it is labelled as such each time.
> - **Several key sources were read at abstract level only**, because of paywalls or 403 errors (SAGE, Springer, Elsevier, some Cambridge pages). Effect sizes are given only where the opened page states them.
> - **Inaccessible.** The Council of Europe sites (coe.int, rm.coe.int) returned 403. CEFR mediation and plurilingual competence are therefore described through the MoE curriculum, which quotes and adapts them [26]. Two Israeli studies could not be opened: Inbar-Lourie (2010) on teachers' L1 choices, and Kedar (2019) on article acquisition by a Hebrew-speaking child. They are not cited for content.
> - **Age and population mismatch.** Much of the Israeli EFL research is on school children (grades 4–12). Lucid's users are 16+, so findings on children are flagged.
> - **Israeli exam rules are moving targets.** NITE is separating English from the Psychometric test from December 2026, and AMIRNET will change from 2027 [31][32]. University cut-offs are set by each institution [33]. All numbers below are as published on 2026-10-30.
>
> **Evidence-strength labels** (same scheme as Team C2):
> - **Strong:** several meta-analyses that agree.
> - **Moderate:** one meta-analysis, or several consistent primary studies, with caveats.
> - **Limited:** one or a few primary studies, abstract-level only, or child populations.
> - **Prediction/framework:** typology, official guidance or expert opinion; not outcome evidence.

## 1. Pronunciation: Hebrew phonology and the predicted English problem areas

### Evidence summary

**Hebrew sound inventory (descriptive).**
- PHOIBLE's inventory from Laufer's *Illustrations of the IPA: Hebrew* (1990) lists 22 consonants and 5 vowels /i e a o u/ [1].
- The consonants include /v/, /h/ and /ʒ/. They do **not** include /w/, /θ/, /ð/ or /ŋ/, and the affricates /tʃ dʒ/ are not listed [1].
- Zaltz & Segal confirm the five-vowel system. Modern Hebrew has "only five vowels" with no phonemic short/long distinction. Vowel duration varies only with stress and word position, from about 70 to 100 ms [2].
- Bat-El et al. transcribe the rhotic as uvular [ʁ] (e.g., *sifʁijá* 'library') [3]. That is a different articulation from the English approximant /ɹ/.

**Predicted segmental difficulties.** These are inferences from the inventory, not measured learner data.
- **Vowels.**
  - English vowel contrasts must be mapped onto five Hebrew categories. Likely collapses: /ɪ/–/iː/ (*ship/sheep*), /ʊ/–/uː/ (*full/fool*), and /æ/–/ʌ/–/e/–/ɑː/ (*cat/cut/bet/cart*).
  - /ɪ/–/iː/ matters most for intelligibility. It has a high functional-load (FL) ranking in both the Catford and Brown lists, and it is the only vowel contrast that Jenkins's Lingua Franca Core prioritises [4].
- **/w/ vs /v/.** Hebrew has /v/ but no /w/ [1], so a /w/→[v] substitution is predicted (*wine* → "vine").
- **/θ ð/.** These are absent from Hebrew [1]. Substitution with nearby sounds is predicted.
  - A practitioner source claims [t d] specifically ("tink", "de") [8]. That claim comes from coaching experience, not from data.
  - No opened source gave a functional-load ranking for /θ/.
- **/ŋ/.** It is absent [1], so *-ing* endings may be realised as [n] or [ŋg]. This is a prediction only.
- **"Final consonant devoicing?"** The evidence does **not** support predicting this for Hebrew speakers.
  - Hebrew allows word-final voiced obstruents. Native final-stressed nouns such as *gamád* 'dwarf' end in /d/ [3].
  - No opened source reports final devoicing in Hebrew-L1 English.
  - If devoicing appears in a learner, a hypothesis to check (not verified here) is influence from another home language, such as Russian, which many Israelis speak.

**Stress and rhythm (descriptive).**
- Hebrew primary stress falls within the last three syllables. **Final stress is the most common pattern**: about 75% of noun stems in one lexicon, and 79% in a 6,811-noun sub-corpus [3].
- Penultimate stress is the default and is common in loanwords. Antepenultimate stress is found mostly in loanwords [3].
- **Duration is the main acoustic cue** to Hebrew stress, and intensity and F0 play only minor roles. There is no acoustic evidence for secondary stress [3].
- English, by contrast, weakens unstressed syllables, often to schwa, and schwa is "the most common English sound" [7].
- The British Council article also notes that learners "will still be understood if they give all vowel sounds their full value". Schwa matters mainly for **listening**: learners who expect full vowels may fail to recognise words they know [7].
- *Inference:* Hebrew speakers may transfer final-stress habits and full unreduced vowels into English. A practitioner source asserts both, misplaced stress and full vowels [8], but no Hebrew-specific study of English stress production was opened.

**Can adult Hebrew speakers learn a vowel contrast their L1 lacks? (experimental)**
- Zaltz & Segal tested 40 Hebrew and 20 Arabic native speakers on Arabic short/long vowels in an ABX task [2].
- Hebrew speakers discriminated length above chance, but less well than Arabic speakers.
- **A single ~20-minute training session, with no feedback**, led to native-like discrimination one week later, and the gain **generalised to untrained nonwords** [2].
- Limits:
  - The contrast was Arabic vowel *length*, not English vowel *quality*. /ɪ/–/iː/ differs mainly in quality, not just duration.
  - The stimuli were nonwords tested in a lab.
  - Participants were Tel Aviv University students with advanced English.
  - Follow-up was one week.

**Phonetic training in general (meta-analytic).**
- Sakai & Moorman (2018) reviewed 25 years of perception-training studies [5]:
  - Perception gains were medium, d = 0.92.
  - Production gains were smaller, d = 0.54.
  - The perception–production correlation was not significant.
  - Obstruents improved more than sonorants or vowels.
- Uchihara, Karas & Thomson (2024) meta-analysed 31 high-variability phonetic training (HVPT) studies [6]:
  - Production effects were small-to-medium. Average gains were 10.5% on trained items and 4.5% on untrained items.
  - There was **no strong support for long-term retention or generalisation** of production gains.
  - Moderators included learner age and context, provision of phonetic information, training duration and time per session.
- Sewell (2021) cautions against applying functional-load rankings mechanically. He argues that low-FL features "may not need to be prioritized" [4].

### Strength of evidence
- **Hebrew phonology:** descriptive and reliable [1][3].
- **Predicted English difficulties for Hebrew speakers:** Prediction/framework only. No Hebrew-L1 English production study was found.
- **Trainability of a missing contrast:** Limited. One lab study, with Arabic rather than English stimuli [2].
- **Perception training improves perception more than production:** Moderate to strong. Two meta-analyses agree on direction, and both report weak retention and generalisation for production [5][6].

### What it means for Lucid
- Build a **perception-first contrast trainer**: HVPT-style, with many voices and minimal pairs. Start with /ɪ–iː/ and /w–v/, then /æ–ʌ–e/ and /ʊ–uː/.
- Treat production gains as modest and uncertain [5][6]. Test retention and untrained items; never report only trained-item scores.
- Score speaking for **intelligibility and comprehensibility**, not native-likeness [4][7].
- Teach schwa and weak forms mainly for **listening** [7].
- Do not ship a final-devoicing module by default.

## 2. Grammar: where Hebrew and English differ

### Evidence summary

**Typological contrasts (WALS, Modern Hebrew; descriptive).**
- **Articles.** Hebrew marks definiteness with an affix (*ha-*). Its indefinite marker is "the same as 'one'" [11]. English *a/an* has no dedicated Hebrew counterpart.
  - Predictions (not measured): omission of *a/an*, and overuse of *one* as an indefinite ("I saw one man...").
- **Copula.** A zero copula for predicate nominals is "possible" in Hebrew. WALS's example uses a pronominal copula: *Moše hu student* 'Moshe is a student' [11].
  - Prediction: *be* omission in the present ("She teacher", "He very tired"). This was not measured for Hebrew-L1 adults.
- **Aspect.** Hebrew has **no grammatical perfective/imperfective marking** [11].
  - Prediction: difficulty with present simple vs present continuous, and with the present perfect.
- **Polar questions.** WALS codes Hebrew as using a **question particle**, not interrogative word order [11].
  - Prediction: English questions without subject–auxiliary inversion or *do*-support ("You like coffee?", "Where you live?").
- **Adjective order.** Hebrew is Noun–Adjective [11].
  - Prediction: occasional post-nominal adjectives at low levels.
- **Word order and morphology.** Russak & Zaretsky describe Hebrew as SVO but with flexible order, against English's relatively fixed SVO. They describe English as "morphologically impoverished" compared with Hebrew's rich morphology [13].

**Learner data.** All of it is on children; none is on adults.
- Russak & Zaretsky (2023) coded verb morphology in obligatory contexts (past tense, present progressive, 3rd-person singular, copula) [12]:
  - The writers were 6th graders, 29 Hebrew-L1 and 29 Arabic-L1, in their 4th year of English.
  - **"Both groups found 3rd person singular the most challenging."**
  - Hebrew speakers showed no transfer of morphological awareness from L1 to EFL. Their EFL morphosyntax was most strongly associated with English reading comprehension [12].
  - The abstract does not rank the copula or progressive for each group.
- In oral narratives by 6th graders, English reading comprehension was the strongest predictor of narrative score for Hebrew speakers (β = 0.40). Hebrew reading comprehension did not correlate with narrative score [13].

**Prepositions, gerund/infinitive, countability.** No Hebrew-specific study was found. Practitioner examples circulate (e.g., "married with" for *married to*), but they come from non-scholarly sites and are **not** cited here as fact.

### Strength of evidence
- **Typological contrasts:** reliable descriptions [11][13].
- **That Hebrew speakers actually make each predicted error, and how often:** Prediction only, except 3rd-person *-s*, which is Limited (one study of children) [12].
- **Prepositions, gerund/infinitive and countability for Hebrew L1:** no evidence located.

### What it means for Lucid
- Build an L1-aware **error taxonomy** for the AI writing and speaking evaluators. Treat its tags as *hypotheses to measure*, not as facts to assert:
  - article *a/an* omission
  - *one*-as-article
  - present copula omission
  - aspect (simple vs continuous, perfect)
  - question formation without inversion
  - 3rd-person *-s*
- Log tag rates per learner and per cohort. Recalibrate the contrastive micro-lessons from Lucid's own data.
- Introduce aspect (simple vs continuous, then perfect) with a short Hebrew-language contrast note, because Hebrew does not mark aspect grammatically [11].
- Give 3rd-person *-s* sustained practice and feedback, not one lesson [12].

## 3. Lexis: collocations, phrasal verbs, look-alike words, dictionaries

### Evidence summary

**Verb–noun collocations (corpus study; Hebrew L1).**
- Laufer & Waldman (2011) built a corpus of about 300,000 words of argumentative and descriptive essays by Hebrew-L1 writers at three proficiency levels, and compared it with the native LOCNESS corpus [14]. The *make/do/take/have* + noun type of collocation falls in this category.
  - Learners at all levels produced **far fewer verb–noun collocations** than native speakers.
  - Collocation counts rose only at the advanced level.
  - Errors, "particularly interlingual ones", **persisted even at advanced levels** [14].
- A conference abstract on the ICCI corpus (which contains Israeli data) reports no significant progress in collocation use across grades 6–12. It is weaker evidence: an abstract, not a peer-reviewed paper [15].

**Phrasal verbs (experiment; Hebrew L1).**
- Dagut & Laufer (1985) is the classic avoidance study [16]. As summarised by Immelman & Cooper [17]:
  - The participants were Hebrew-speaking EFL students with at least seven years of school English.
  - Multiple-choice, translation and memorisation tests showed they **preferred one-word verbs to phrasal verbs**.
  - The authors attributed this to Hebrew lacking the structure.
- The finding is **contested** [17]:
  - Hulstijn & Marchena (1989) argued that the avoidance could be semantic, not structural.
  - Liao & Fukuya (2004) criticised the earlier work for not controlling proficiency adequately.
- The original article's opening also cites Levenston (1971) on Hebrew speakers under-using English structures that lack Hebrew equivalents [16].

**Look-alike words ("synforms") (descriptive/theory).**
- Laufer (1988) classified learner confusions between similar word forms into ten categories [18]:
  - affix differences on a shared root
  - one-phoneme differences
  - same consonants with different vowels
- The last category is noteworthy for readers of a consonant-based script (see §4).
- No peer-reviewed list of **Hebrew–English false friends** was found. Popular lists exist but were not verified.

**Dictionaries (experiment; Israeli learners).**
- Laufer & Hadar (1997) compared monolingual, bilingual and "bilingualised" dictionaries for comprehension and production of 15 low-frequency words, with 123 high-school and university learners [19].
- They concluded that "different dictionaries may be suitable for users with different abilities in dictionary use" [19].
- The opened abstract does not say which type won overall.

**Contrastive analysis and translation (experiment).**
- Laufer & Girsai (2008) compared three high-school groups sharing an L1, with time-on-task equal across groups [20]:
  - meaning-focused instruction
  - non-contrastive form-focused instruction
  - **contrastive analysis and translation** (L2→L1 and L1→L2 translation, with teacher feedback contrasting target items with their L1 equivalents)
- The translation group **significantly outperformed both other groups** on active and passive recall of 10 words and 10 collocations, immediately and one week later [20].
- The opened abstract does not name the shared L1. The study was run by University of Haifa researchers.

### Strength of evidence
- **Collocation weakness persists into advanced levels in Hebrew-L1 writing:** Moderate. One large learner-corpus study [14], plus weaker corroboration [15].
- **Phrasal-verb avoidance:** Limited and contested [16][17].
- **Contrastive translation tasks beat equal-time alternatives for vocabulary:** Limited. One quasi-experiment with short-term follow-up [20].
- **Hebrew–English false friends:** no evidence base located.

### What it means for Lucid
- Make **collocations a first-class SRS unit** (verb+noun, adjective+noun), not only single words. Start at A2, and keep collocation feedback active in the Writing Lab up to C2 [14].
- Track phrasal-verb use per 1,000 words in learner writing and speech. Nudge learners who avoid them, but do not penalise accurate one-word alternatives in formal registers [16][17].
- Include short **two-way translation tasks with contrastive feedback** in vocabulary sets [20].
- Add a "confusable forms" drill type for synforms [18].
- Hebrew–English false friends must be **curated and reviewed by bilingual experts** before shipping.
- Offer a **bilingualised entry** (Hebrew gloss plus English definition and examples), with a user toggle [19].

## 4. Writing and orthography: capitals, vowels, direction, punctuation

### Evidence summary

**Script facts (standard).**
- Hebrew script "is written from right to left". Vowel points "are usually omitted", except in liturgical and special texts. Five letters have word-final forms [21].
- The Unicode Standard's list of scripts that have case names Latin, Greek, Coptic, Cyrillic, Glagolitic, Armenian and others. **Hebrew is not among them** [22]. Hebrew therefore gives no L1 model for capital letters.
- No study of capitalisation errors by Hebrew-L1 writers was found, so the predicted error (missing capitals in names, *I*, sentence starts) remains unmeasured.

**Spelling (Hebrew-L1 EFL; children and adolescents).**
- Russak & Kahn-Horwitz (2015) studied 233 Hebrew-L1 students in grades 5, 8 and 10 [23]:
  - Good and poor spellers differed at all grades on novel phonemes and English orthographic conventions.
  - "Spelling accuracy for most conventions did not improve after the eighth grade."
  - **Hebrew spelling was among the strongest predictors of English spelling.**
- A 9-year longitudinal study (grades 4, 9, 12) found that early English spelling strongly predicted later English spelling. Grade-4 L1 literacy predicted grade-9 EFL spelling better than English word recognition did [24].
- For comparison, Arabic-L1 learners, whose script is also a consonant-based abjad, spelled vowels less accurately than consonants, struggled with novel phonemes, and found digraphs hard [25]. This is an analogue, not Hebrew data.

**Punctuation, run-ons, paragraphing.** No Hebrew-specific peer-reviewed evidence was found. Any claim that Hebrew comma conventions cause English run-on sentences is unverified.

**Bagrut writing is short.** This shows how little extended English writing many school leavers have practised.
- The 3-point Bagrut composition is **70–90 words** (opinion, description, informal letter) [29].
- The 5-point (B2) composition is **120–140 words** (opinion, for-and-against, description, email, story, formal letter), with grammar and spelling deductions [28].

### Strength of evidence
- **Script facts:** Strong (standards documents) [21][22].
- **Hebrew-L1 EFL spelling plateaus and L1 spelling predicts EFL spelling:** Moderate for school-age learners [23][24]. Generalisation to adults is untested.
- **Capitalisation, punctuation and run-on transfer:** no direct evidence.

### What it means for Lucid
- The Pre-A1 to A2 track needs explicit **capitalisation and English vowel-spelling** instruction, with automatic checks in every writing input.
- The placement test should include a short **spelling probe** for low levels [23][24].
- The Writing Lab must take learners from **paragraph-length (70–140 words) to multi-paragraph argumentative essays**, because the Bagrut caps practice at 140 words [28][29].
- Punctuation and run-on feedback should rely on general writing-feedback evidence (Team C2) plus Lucid's own error logs, not on assumed Hebrew transfer.

## 5. Israeli context: curriculum, Bagrut, Psychometric/AMIRNET, university exemption

### Evidence summary

**MoE English Curriculum 2020: CEFR-based, localised.**
- The curriculum "was designed in alignment with" the CEFR. Localisation addresses Israel's "ethnically and linguistically heterogeneous population" [27].
- The level correspondence (Table 1) is [26]:

| English Curriculum 2020 | CEFR | Revised Curriculum 2018 | Exit point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-Basic User | Pre-A1 | Pre-foundation | — |
| Basic User I | A1 | Foundation | elementary |
| Basic User II | A2 | Intermediate | junior high **and 3-point Bagrut** |
| Independent User I | B1 | Proficiency | **4-point Bagrut** |
| Independent User II | B2 | Proficiency | **5-point Bagrut** |

- The grammar bands label the 3-point level "Basic User II+ (A2+)" [26].
- The 5-point handbook calls B2 "the expected proficiency at the end of High School" and the exit level for the 5-point Bagrut [28].
- **Vocabulary targets** are given as approximate, cumulative counts [26]:

| Level | Receptive | Productive |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-A1 | 200 | 200 |
| A1 | 1,400 | 1,200 |
| A2 | 3,400 | 2,000 |
| B1 | 4,300 | 2,500 |
| B2 | 5,400 | 3,000 |

- These lexical bands are frequency-based and include chunks [26].
- The curriculum includes CEFR **mediation** and **plurilingual/pluricultural competence** components [26].
- Context from a study of children: formal English starts in grade 3, with 2×45 minutes a week, rising to 4 lessons a week in grades 4–6. The authors also note that many teachers use pupils' L1 in class [13].

**Bagrut structure** [28][29].
- **5-point:** Module E (27%), Module F literature (26%, school-internal), Module G (27%), and COBE computerised oral exam (20%).
  - Module G = reading (one 450–500-word text, 60%) plus one composition (40%) in 1 h 45 min.
  - No dictionary is allowed in Module E.
  - An "English Academization Program" (a university-level course in grade 12) can replace Module G.
- **3-point:** Modules A, B (literature) and C, plus an oral exam.
  - Module A applies no grammar or spelling deductions.
- **4-point:** maps to B1 [26]. Its handbook was not opened.

**Psychometric Entrance Test (PET) English: historical format.**
- NITE's examinee guide (practice-test 5E edition, undated) gives the format [30]:
  - English sections contain **sentence completions, restatements and reading comprehension**, all four-option multiple choice.
  - Each section has two reading texts with five or six questions each.
  - The English area is scored on a **50–150 scale**.
  - In the total PET score (200–800), verbal and quantitative reasoning each carry **twice the weight of English** [30].
- **Change from December 2026:** the Council for Higher Education (CHE) decided that English must assess more than reading. From the Winter (December) 2026 administration, PET has only verbal and quantitative domains, and **"The level of proficiency in English is evaluated only in the AMIRNET test"** [31].
  - Taking an English placement test becomes an admission requirement.
  - Each institution decides how to use the score: as part of admission, for placement only, or as a cut-off [31].

**AMIRNET** (formerly AMIR/AMIRAM) [32][33].
- It is computerised and **adaptive**, takes about 50 minutes, and is scored on **50–150**. Scores are equivalent to former PET-English, AMIR and AMIRAM scores.
- Current sections are sentence completions, reading comprehension and restatements. An experimental writing task may appear.
- NITE says changes from **2027** will add **listening and writing**, with **speaking "further in the future"**. This follows the CHE decision of 17 December 2019 [32].
- "Each academic institution makes its own independent decisions" based on the score [33].

**University English levels: example (Tel Aviv University)** [34].

| Score (PET-English / AMIR(AM)) | Level |
|---|---|
| 50–69 | Pre-Basic A |
| 70–84 | Pre-Basic B |
| 85–94 | Basic A |
| 95–99 | Basic B |
| 100–119 | Advanced A |
| 120–133 | Advanced B |
| **134+** | **Exempt ("ptor")** |

- First-year BA students below exemption must reach it within about one year; some units allow three semesters [34].
- A separate TAU table maps these bands to CEFR [35]:
  - 134+ → B2/C1/C2
  - 120–133 → B1+/B2
  - 100–119 → A2+/B1
  - 85–99 → A2/A2+, the "minimal grade for acceptance"
- The page extraction was partly garbled and the asterisks are unexplained, so treat this mapping as **approximate and institution-specific** [35].

**CHE reform (2019).**
- Approved 17 December 2019 [36]:
  - a move to **four-skill, CEFR-based** English study and assessment by 2024/25
  - **at least two English courses** for undergraduates starting from 2021/22
  - an MoE commitment to align matriculation exams to the same four-skill format
  - promotion of English Medium Instruction
- An earlier review of Israeli ELT research (2004–2009) rated its overall quality as high. It noted themes including lexis teaching, dyslexia, and students who arrive already knowing some English from home or travel [37].

### Strength of evidence
- **Official structures and numbers:** Strong as descriptions of policy [26]–[36]. They change often (AMIRNET 2027; PET split December 2026), and cut-offs vary by institution.
- **The CEFR mapping of Bagrut levels is official** [26][27]. The opened sources do not describe any external validation of that mapping.
- **The CEFR mapping of university bands is a single institution's table** [35].

### What it means for Lucid
- Lucid's level ladder lines up with the MoE ladder, Pre-A1 → B2, and then continues to C1–C2.
- Show **approximate** Bagrut and AMIRNET equivalence labels so Israeli users can orient themselves. They must be labelled as unofficial until validated.
- Exam practice should cover the AMIRNET item types (sentence completion, restatement, reading comprehension) now, with listening and writing from 2027 [32].
- The academic track should build all four skills, matching the CHE reform [36].

## 6. Own-language (L1) use, translanguaging, and gradual withdrawal of L1 support

### Evidence summary

**State of the art and teacher practice.**
- Hall & Cook (2012) review how the long-standing monolingual assumption is being re-evaluated. They cover the arguments, the empirical studies of how much and why own-language use occurs, and the notion of "optimal" use [38].
- Their British Council survey (2013) covered **2,785 teachers in 111 countries**, a non-probability, self-report sample [39]:
  - 72% use the learners' language at least sometimes to explain unclear meanings: 30.1% sometimes, 25.7% often, 16.2% always.
  - 61.5% explain vocabulary via the L1 at least sometimes, and 58.1% explain grammar that way.
  - 73.5% "allow own-language use only at certain points of the lesson".
  - Own-language use was **significantly more frequent with beginner to pre-intermediate classes**.
  - **Higher-level learners were more likely to expect English-only classes** (61.4% vs 40.2% agreeing).
  - Some respondents said L1 use suits "the first few weeks of a course before being phased out or reduced over time" [39].
- Israeli policy is mixed:
  - The MoE curriculum values "maximum exposure to the target language" but also learners' use of "linguistic resources (L1 and other languages) when it helps them promote comprehension" [26].
  - Its vocabulary foreword (by Batia Laufer) endorses "a judicious exploitation of L1", "occasional translations", "brief explanations of L1-L2 differences" and practice of L1-induced difficulties [26].

**Experimental and meta-analytic evidence.**
- **L1 vs L2 glosses.**
  - Yanagisawa, Webb & Uchihara (2020): 42 studies, 359 effect sizes, N = 3,802. "L1 glosses yielded greater learning than L2 glosses" [41]:
    - There was **no interaction with proficiency**.
    - Multiple-choice glosses were most effective.
    - Glossaries and in-text glosses were least effective.
  - Kim, Lee & Lee (2024): 26 studies, N = 2,189. L1 glossing beat L2 glossing overall, **g = .33** [42]:
    - The effect was clearest on immediate vocabulary tests and less clear on delayed tests and reading comprehension.
    - The effect was **largest for beginners**.
  - The two meta-analyses disagree on whether proficiency moderates the effect [41][42].
- **Teacher L1 explanations.**
  - Lee & Macaro (2013): Korean EFL, 443 children and 286 university adults. **Both** groups benefited from L1-linked vocabulary instruction compared with English-only, and the young learners benefited more [43].
  - Tian & Macaro (2012): 80 Chinese university students, randomised, plus a 37-student control. They report "initial evidence" that teacher codeswitching may beat English-only explanations, with no clear proficiency moderation [44].
- **Contrastive translation tasks** outperformed equal-time alternatives [20] (§3).
- **Translanguaging.**
  - Wang & Zhang (2026, *Language Teaching*) meta-analysed 40 studies with 108 effect sizes and 3,145 learners. The pooled effect of translanguaging pedagogy on L2 achievement was **g = 1.014** (95% CrI 0.748–1.291) [40]. Caveats:
    - 85.8% of the variance was between studies.
    - Egger's test showed asymmetry (p < .0001), although other checks suggested no severe bias.
    - **Only 3 of 40 studies had delayed post-tests.**
    - **Adult learners showed a small, non-credible effect (g = 0.263, k = 6)**, while tertiary students showed the largest (g = 1.326, k = 44).
    - Speaking had the largest effect (g = 1.364) and vocabulary the lowest (g = 0.693) [40].
  - Cenoz & Gorter's *Pedagogical Translanguaging* separates **planned (pedagogical)** from **spontaneous** translanguaging [45].
    - Their reviewer doubts that positive cross-linguistic influence holds for **typologically distant** pairs [45]. Hebrew and English are such a pair.

**Gradual withdrawal.**
- No study was found that tests a specific *schedule* for fading L1 support.
- The convergent indirect evidence:
  - L1 support helps most at low levels [42][43].
  - Teachers already use less L1 as levels rise [39].
  - Higher-level learners expect English-only [39].
- Together these support **level-contingent fading**, but the thresholds have to be set and tested by Lucid itself.

### Strength of evidence
- **L1 glosses beat L2 glosses for vocabulary:** Moderate to strong. Two meta-analyses agree on direction; the effect is small and mostly immediate [41][42].
- **Teacher L1 explanations help vocabulary:** Limited to moderate. A few randomised or quasi-experimental studies, none Hebrew-specific [43][44].
- **Translanguaging improves L2 achievement:** Moderate overall but **weak for adults and for durability** [40]. Theory for typologically distant languages is contested [45].
- **Fading schedule:** inference only.

### What it means for Lucid
- Hebrew is a legitimate support language, especially for glosses, contrastive notes and meta-cognitive guidance.
- Hebrew support should **fade with level**, and must not leak into the target-language practice itself.
- Do not market translanguaging as proven for adult self-study.

## 7. Right-to-left and bidirectional text in a mixed Hebrew–English interface

### Evidence summary (standards and guidance)

**Structure** [47].
- Set `dir="rtl"` on the `html` element whenever the overall document direction is right-to-left.
- Declare `lang` as well, and do not assume that language implies direction.
- **"Do not use CSS to apply base direction"**. Direction belongs in the markup.
- Use CSS **logical properties** (start/end) so the layout mirrors correctly.
- Use `dir="auto"` for runtime text of unknown direction. Detection works from the first strong character and can misfire on mixed content.
- A Latin-initial title on an RTL page can display wrongly. Prefix it with `&rlm;`.

**Inline runs** [46].
- "Tightly wrap *every* opposite-direction phrase in markup that sets its base direction."
- Use `bdi` or `dir="auto"` for inserted text of unknown direction.
- Unwrapped phrases cause **spill-over**: adjacent numbers and punctuation display on the wrong side.
- Prefer markup to invisible Unicode controls, except where markup is impossible (e.g., `title`, attribute values).
- Use `bdo` only for special cases.

**Hebrew-specific gaps** (W3C Hebrew Gap Analysis, May 2025 draft) [48].
- Base direction can be lost when strings pass between applications. The `dirname` form attribute "isn't fully interoperable".
- Support for logical CSS **shorthands** (`margin-inline`, `margin-block`) is weak.
- `:dir()` is described as not supported by all engines, although the heading marks it as fixed.
- Isolating characters (RLI/LRI/PDI/FSI) are needed where markup is unavailable.
- Browsers ignore or mis-render diacritics coloured differently from their base letters. This matters if Lucid ever highlights niqqud [48].

**Learner-facing research.** No empirical study was found on Hebrew speakers' learning outcomes in mixed-direction interfaces. The recommendations below are engineering guidance, not learning evidence.

### Strength of evidence
- **Rendering correctness:** Strong (W3C guidance and standards).
- **Pedagogical impact of bidi bugs:** no evidence. Treat it as a usability and quality requirement.

### What it means for Lucid
- Every English learning item must render LTR and isolated inside Hebrew UI chrome.
- Word order matters in English exercises, so **no English content may ever be mirrored**.
- Bidi correctness is a release-blocking test category for Hebrew UI.

## What not to build

- **No claim, in product copy or content, that "Swan & Smith say Hebrew speakers…".** The book has no Hebrew chapter [9].
- **No universal Hebrew error list presented as fact.** The article, copula, aspect and question predictions [11] are hypotheses to measure. Prepositions, gerund/infinitive, countability and punctuation have no Hebrew-specific evidence.
- **No default final-devoicing drills** for Hebrew speakers [3].
- **No "accent reduction to native" scoring**, and no blocking of progress on /θ ð/ or uvular /r/ [4][7].
- **No perception trainer that reports only trained-item gains.** Generalisation and retention are the weak points [5][6].
- **No strict English-only immersion from day one for Pre-A1–A1 adults** [41][42][43].
- **No permanent Hebrew crutch at B2+.** Higher-level learners expect English-only [39], and L1 benefits shrink with proficiency [42].
- **No marketing of "translanguaging" as proven for adult self-learners.** The adult effect was small and non-credible, and durability is unknown [40].
- **No Hebrew–English false-friend or loanword list from popular websites** without expert review.
- **No AMIRNET or Bagrut score predictor** before validation. **No reuse of NITE or MoE exam items**; write original items. **No single national exemption threshold**: institutions decide [31][33][34].
- **No transfer of Arabic-L1 Israeli learners' error profiles to Hebrew-L1 users** [25].
- **No mirroring of English learning content, no CSS-based direction, and no reliance on invisible bidi control characters inside content** [46][47][48].
