# Team C1 — Memory, practice and retention science for language learning

> **Method.** Desk research run on **2026-10-30** for Lucid, a premium English course for Hebrew-speaking learners aged 16+ that runs from Pre-A1 to C1–C2 argumentative writing and includes a Real-Life English track. Every factual claim below carries a citation [n] to a page, record or PDF that was **opened in this session** with a fetch tool. Search-result snippets were used only to find sources and are never cited.
>
> Where a full-text PDF could be retrieved, the key numbers (effect sizes, table values, coverage figures) were checked against the extracted text, not just the fetch tool's summary. ## 1. Spaced repetition and the distributed-practice effect

### Evidence summary

- **Core effect (general memory, strong).**
  - Cepeda et al.'s meta-analysis pooled 839 assessments from 317 experiments in 184 articles. It found that the gap between study sessions that gives the best retention grows as the retention interval grows [1].
  - Dunlosky et al.'s review of ten learning techniques rated **distributed practice** and **practice testing** the only two "high utility" techniques [5].
- **Size and shape of the effect over long intervals (experiment, strong).** Cepeda et al. (2008) taught facts to over 1,350 people online, with study gaps of up to 3.5 months and final tests up to a year later [2].
  - For any fixed test delay, final performance first rose and then slowly fell as the study gap widened [2].
  - The optimal gap was about 20% of the test delay for delays of a few weeks, falling to about 5% for a one-year delay [2]. The PubMed abstract gives 20–40% for one week and 5–10% for one year [2].
  - For retention intervals of 7, 35, 70 and 350 days, the best gaps tested for recall were 1, 11, 21 and 21 days [2].
  - Holding study time constant, the optimal gap gave a 64% gain in final recall (d = 1.1) over a zero gap [2].
  - The authors note that a gap that is too long costs much less than a gap that is too short [2].
- **L2 vocabulary specifically (review of experiments, moderate to strong for list learning).**
  - Kim & Webb's 2022 meta-analysis covered 37 experimental L2 studies. It confirmed a positive effect of spacing in general, but effects differed by L2 area, learner and practice activity, and some analyses were inconclusive for lack of studies [4][3].
  - Serrano (2022) reviewed 47 studies and concluded there is **one robust finding**: when learning words from lists in one session, spaced repetitions beat massed ones [4]. Learners should go through the whole list before repeating individual items [4]. Spreading vocabulary study over several sessions also beats a single session [4].
- **Lag effects are inconsistent (contested).** It is not settled *how long* the gaps between sessions should be for L2 learning.
  - **Classrooms.** Studies have often failed to show that longer lags are better. Rogers & Cheung found a 1-day lag beat an 8-day lag in one study and found no difference in a replication [4]. Küpper-Tetzel et al. found the 1-day lag best at 7 days, but no short-versus-long difference at 5 weeks [4].
  - **Expanding versus equal spacing.** Results are mixed: Nakata (2015) favoured expanding spacing within one session, while multi-session studies conflict [4].
- **Grammar and skills (limited, mixed).**
  - Receptive grammar knowledge measured by grammaticality judgement benefited from longer lags in some classroom studies (Bird 2010; Rogers 2015) [4].
  - Productive and proceduralised grammar was better with *shorter* lags in Suzuki & DeKeyser's studies [4].
  - Low-proficiency learners and learners who struggled during training did better with shorter lags. A longer lag was a desirable difficulty only for higher-proficiency learners (Serfaty & Serrano 2022) [4].
- **Program level (limited).**
  - Intensive programmes (more hours, concentrated) tended to outperform "drip-feed" schedules of one to two hours a week, which "does not lead to advanced L2 skills" (Lightbown, cited in [4]).
  - Program-level "intensity" means total time on the language, not massed repetition of one item. Many of the studies are small [4].
- **Long-term L2 vocabulary (one small classic study).** Bahrick et al. (1993) followed 4 participants over years. Spacing sessions out to 56 days improved foreign-language vocabulary retention. Cepeda et al. note that the design trained to a fixed criterion, which confounds study time with spacing (described in [2]).

### Strength of evidence

| Claim | Strength |
|---|---|
| The spacing effect for verbal and list learning | **Strong**: meta-analyses [1][3] and large experiments [2] |
| Spacing for L2 vocabulary from lists | **Strong** [4] |
| An optimal gap that depends on the retention interval | **Strong** in general memory [1][2]; **moderate** for L2 [4] |
| Exact optimal lags for L2 grammar or fluency, and expanding versus uniform schedules | **Contested** [4] |

### What it means for Lucid

- Every vocabulary item, chunk and grammar point needs **scheduled re-encounters across days**, not more repetitions within one day.
- The scheduler should **err towards longer gaps** for receptive knowledge, because gaps that are too long cost less than gaps that are too short [2].
- For productive grammar and for **lower-proficiency** learners, it should **shorten** lags [4].

## 2. Spaced-repetition algorithms: SM-2 versus FSRS (and HLR)

### Evidence summary

**SM-2 (1987–1990, rule-based).**
- **How it works.** Wozniak's SM-2 sets I(1) = 1 day, I(2) = 6 days, then I(n) = I(n−1) × EF [6]:
  - Each item starts with an easiness factor EF = 2.5.
  - EF is updated after every review from a 0–5 grade and is never allowed below 1.3.
  - Any grade below 3 restarts the item's repetitions from scratch [6].
- **SM-2 has no probability model**, so it cannot target a chosen retention level.
- **Anki's SM-2 variant** [8]:
  - Four answer buttons instead of six grades.
  - Starting ease of 250%.
  - Again lowers ease by 20 percentage points, Hard by 15, and Easy raises it by 15, with a floor of 130%.
  - Lateness is factored in.

**FSRS (Free Spaced Repetition Scheduler).**
- **Memory model.** FSRS describes each memory with three variables [9]:
  - **Retrievability R**, the probability of recall.
  - **Stability S**, defined as the interval at which R = 90%.
  - **Difficulty D**, on a 1–10 scale.
- **Grades and update rules.** Ratings run 1–4 (Again, Hard, Good, Easy). FSRS-6 uses a power-law forgetting curve R(t,S) = (1 + factor·t/S)^(−w20), whose decay exponent is trainable per user [9]. The stability gain after a successful review is larger when S is small, when D is low and when R was low at review time, so spacing pays off [9].
- **Desired retention.**
  - Anki's default is **90%**, which the manual calls "a good balance of retention and workload" [7].
  - Workload rises steeply as retention approaches 100%, and the manual advises staying **below 97%** [7].
- **Parameter fitting.**
  - Parameters are fitted to the user's review history. Monthly re-optimisation "is sufficient" [7].
  - FSRS may perform poorly when the user has "less than a few hundred" reviews [7].
- **Learning steps** should be short, same-day and as few as possible, because repeating a card several times in one day "adds little to long-term memory" [7].
- **"Hard" is a passing grade.** Pressing it after a failure inflates intervals [7].

**Benchmark evidence (documentation/benchmark, not peer-reviewed).** The open-spaced-repetition benchmark uses about 727 million reviews from 10,000 Anki users. After filtering, it evaluates 349,923,850 reviews from 9,999 collections, excluding same-day reviews [10].

Source for all rows: [10]. A neural RWKV model trained across users scores better still (log loss 0.2773), but the README notes it can make "unintuitive predictions" [10]. **SM-2 is not in the current README table** [10]. A separate benchmark write-up reports that FSRS-6 gives lower log loss than Anki's SM-2 "for 99.6% of users". That comparison needed "a not-so-rigorous interval-to-probability converter" because SM-2 does not predict recall, and its author states no truly fair comparison is possible [11]. The Anki FAQ claims FSRS needs fewer reviews than SM-2 for the same retention and is "roughly on par with SM-17" in preliminary tests [8]; these are vendor and documentation claims.

**Other trainable schedulers.**
- **HLR (Duolingo).** Duolingo's half-life regression cut error in predicting recall by "45%+" against baseline schedulers and raised daily student engagement by 12% in an operational test [14]. On the Anki benchmark data, however, HLR is worse than the constant AVG baseline in log loss [10]. A model that wins on one platform's data does not necessarily transfer.
- **SSP-MMC (MaiMemo).** MaiMemo, a Chinese vocabulary app, published SSP-MMC at KDD 2022. It optimises review schedules on about 220 million memory logs, and the code is MIT-licensed [13].

**Implementation availability.** `ts-fsrs` is an MIT-licensed TypeScript library [12]:
- It supports FSRS 3, 4.5, 5, 6 and 7, and defaults to FSRS-7.
- It ships a separate optimiser package for training parameters from review logs.
- v6 and the optimiser package require Node.js ≥ 24 [12].

### Strength of evidence

| Claim | Strength |
|---|---|
| FSRS predicts recall better than HLR and SM-2-derived estimates on Anki data | **Moderate**: large data, transparent, but non-peer-reviewed and run by the FSRS developers [10][11] |
| FSRS produces better *learning outcomes* than SM-2 | **No direct experimental evidence found**: no randomised comparison of learning outcomes was located |
| Desired retention around 0.90 is a sensible default | **Guidance-level** [7] |

### What it means for Lucid

- Use **FSRS through `ts-fsrs` (MIT)**, not SM-2 or a home-grown algorithm.
- **Store raw review logs** (timestamp, rating, latency, exercise type) so the scheduler can be refitted or swapped as models change [10].
- Rate reviews **objectively** from the learner's answer, not with self-graded buttons.
- Start new users on **default parameters**. On the benchmark, FSRS-7 with default parameters beats the constant baseline (log loss 0.3620 versus 0.3945) and about equals per-user-fitted FSRS-4.5 (0.3625) [10].

## 3. Retrieval practice, the testing effect and active recall

### Evidence summary

- **General memory (meta-analyses, strong).**
  - Rowland (2014) analysed 159 effect sizes from 61 studies. Testing beat restudy with **g = 0.50** (95% CI 0.42–0.58) [15].
  - **Feedback.** Effects were larger with feedback (**g = 0.73**) than without (**g = 0.39**) [15].
  - **Retention interval.** Effects were larger at retention intervals of **≥ 1 day (g = 0.69)** than under 1 day (g = 0.41) [15].
  - **Test format.** A **cued-recall** initial test (g = 0.61) beat a **recognition** test (g = 0.29) [15].
  - **Low success without feedback is useless.** With no feedback and initial test success of 50% or less, the testing effect was **g = 0.03**, i.e. none [15].
  - Ordering items mixed or blocked made little difference (g = 0.49 versus 0.46) [15].
- **Real classrooms (meta-analysis, strong).**
  - Yang et al. (2021) pooled 222 studies with 48,478 students and found a medium benefit of quizzing, **g = 0.499** [16].
  - The benefit was moderated by corrective feedback, number of test repetitions, format consistency and how well the test matched the material [16].
  - Adesope et al. (2017) found practice tests "more beneficial for learning than restudying and all other comparison conditions" [17].
  - A summary of Adesope et al. by learning scientists (secondary) reports [18]:
    - effects in classrooms as large as in the lab;
    - the largest effects for secondary-school students;
    - stronger benefits when the final test came more than a day later;
    - only a slight added benefit from feedback in that dataset.
- **L2 vocabulary experiments (strong for paired associates).**
  - **Testing beats restudy for foreign words.** Karpicke & Roediger (2008) taught 40 Swahili–English word pairs [19].
    - Continuing to *test* already-recalled items led to about 80% recall a week later.
    - Dropping them from further testing gave only 36% recall if they were still restudied, and 33% if they were dropped from study too.
    - Students predicted about 50% recall in every condition, and their predictions did not track actual performance [19].
  - **Feedback after errors is what counts.** Pashler et al. (2005) taught 258 people Luganda–English pairs [20].
    - Giving the correct answer after an *error* raised final retention one week later by 494%.
    - Feedback after *correct* answers made little difference [20].
  - **More retrievals help, but with diminishing returns per minute.** Nakata (2017) tested 98 Japanese EFL learners on 16 word pairs [22].
    - Five or seven retrievals in one session gave higher scores than one or three.
    - Per unit of study time, a single retrieval gave the largest gain [22].
- **Failed attempts help when followed by the answer (experiment, moderate).** Across six experiments, guessing before seeing the answer improved later recall compared with simply reading the question and answer together, even when the guesses were wrong [21].
- **L2 intentional vocabulary activities (meta-analysis).** Webb, Yanagisawa & Uchihara (2020) pooled 100 effect sizes from 22 studies of flashcards, word lists, writing and fill-in-the-blanks [23].
  - Gains were substantial on immediate tests but **fell considerably on delayed tests**.
  - Learning "is far from guaranteed", and outcomes varied with direction of learning (L1→L2 or L2→L1) and place of study [23].

### Strength of evidence

- **Testing effect: strong** in general memory [15][16][17] and for L2 paired associates [19][20][22].
- **The size of the benefit for real-world L2 proficiency** (fluency, writing) is **untested** in the sources opened.
- The classroom meta-analysis [16] supports transfer beyond the lab.

### What it means for Lucid

- Make **recall with immediate corrective feedback** the default for every practice item. Recognition (multiple choice) is a stepping stone only.
- **Never run unscored or unfeedbacked retrieval at low success rates.** It does nothing [15].
- **Do not let learners remove items by self-judgement.** Predictions of one's own recall are unreliable [19].

## 4. Interleaving versus blocking (including L2 grammar)

### Evidence summary

- **Meta-analysis (strong, with caveats).** Brunmair & Richter (2019) analysed 59 studies with 238 effect sizes and found an overall interleaving benefit of **g = 0.42** [27].
  - **By material.** The benefit was largest for paintings (g = 0.67) and small for maths (g = 0.34) [27].
  - **Words reversed it.** For word materials, **blocking beat interleaving (g = −0.39)** [27].
  - **When interleaving helps.** It helps most when categories look alike to each other but members within a category vary [27].
  - **Authors' cautions.** They advise caution "especially for expository texts and words", while noting that the few word studies may reflect task type rather than words as such [27].
- **L2 grammar experiments (moderate).**
  - **Nakata & Suzuki (2019).** In a study with 115 Japanese EFL learners on five English structures, interleaving produced the **most errors during practice** but was **better than blocking one week later**. Learners with lower pretest scores benefited more [28].
  - **Pan et al. (2019): Spanish verb conjugation.**
    - Interleaving did **not** beat blocking within a single session (Experiments 1–2).
    - It **substantially improved** performance on a one-week delayed test when practice ran over two weekly sessions (Experiments 3–4) [29].
  - **Other L2 studies (via Serrano [4]):**
    - Suzuki & Sunada (2020) found a **hybrid** schedule (blocked, then interleaved) best for relative pronouns.
    - Suzuki et al. (2022b) found an interleaving advantage for oral production of relative clauses that had disappeared by 7 days.
    - For **oral fluency**, blocked task repetition gave more fluent speech (Suzuki 2021).
    - For **French pronunciation rules**, blocking beat interleaving (Carpenter & Mueller 2013).
- **General review.** Dunlosky et al. rate interleaved practice "moderate utility", promising but with limited evidence [5].

### Strength of evidence

| Claim | Strength |
|---|---|
| Interleaving for discriminating confusable categories (grammar forms that compete) | **Moderate** [27][28][29] |
| Interleaving for learning new word meanings | **Not supported; possibly harmful** [27] |
| Blocked practice for fluency and pronunciation rules | **Limited** (small studies via [4]) |

### What it means for Lucid

- **Introduce** each new grammar point in a **blocked** sequence: worked examples, then guided practice.
- **Review** it **interleaved** with the structures it competes with, across days and sessions.
- **Do not** interleave brand-new vocabulary categories as a teaching technique.
- Keep **fluency and pronunciation** drills blocked, with a small number of repetitions.

## 5. Deliberate practice and skill acquisition

### Evidence summary

- **Definition and claim tested.** Macnamara et al. (2014) define deliberate practice as "engagement in structured activities created specifically to improve performance in a domain". Their meta-analysis tested whether differences in performance "largely reflect" differences in the amount of such practice [30].
- **How much it explains.** Deliberate practice explained [30]:
  - **26%** of variance in performance for games
  - **21%** for music
  - **18%** for sports
  - **4%** for education
  - **under 1%** for professions

  The authors conclude it is "not as important as has been argued" [30]. Ericsson et al.'s 1993 paper was not opened; it is described here only through this test of it.
- **L2 skill acquisition (limited, via review).**
  - Practice schedule matters differently for declarative and proceduralised knowledge [4]:
    - shorter lags favoured faster, automatised production;
    - longer lags favoured retention of declarative knowledge.
  - Massed task repetition was a "double-edged sword": it reduced pauses but slowed articulation rate and increased verbatim repetition [4].

### Strength of evidence

- **Modest** for deliberate practice as an explanation of educational outcomes [30].
- **Limited** for specific L2 practice schedules [4].

### What it means for Lucid

- Build targeted practice of each learner's *own* error patterns (an error log that feeds the adaptive engine), with feedback.
- **Do not market "deliberate practice" as a cure-all.** In education it explains little of the variance between people [30].

## 6. Cognitive load theory: worked examples, split attention and element interactivity

### Evidence summary

- **Theory (framework).** Cognitive load theory (CLT) holds that new information passes through a working memory of limited capacity and duration. Information already stored in long-term memory escapes those limits [31]. The NSW CESE report puts working memory at "about four 'chunks'" [32].
- **Kinds of load [32]:**
  - **Intrinsic load** comes from the complexity of the material relative to the learner's prior knowledge, i.e. element interactivity. It can be reduced by sequencing from simple to complex, or by part-whole teaching.
  - **Extraneous load** comes from poor design.
- **Effects relevant to Lucid (guidance/review summarising many RCTs):**
  - **Worked examples.** Novices learn more from studying worked examples than from solving equivalent problems. CESE cites a meta-analytic effect size of 0.52 (Crissman 2006) [32].
  - **Expertise reversal.** Heavy use of worked examples becomes ineffective or counter-productive as expertise grows, so guidance should fade [32].
  - **Split attention.** Sources that must be integrated mentally (e.g. a word and its gloss in separate places) should be physically integrated [32].
  - **Redundancy.** The same information repeated in several forms, or non-essential information, can impair learning [32].
  - **Modality.** Splitting information across the visual and auditory channels can reduce load [32].
- **Critiques recorded by CESE itself [32]:**
  - The three load types are hard to separate and measure.
  - Most studies do not measure load directly.
  - Short lab tasks may not generalise.
  - Most RCTs are in maths, science and technology, with "far less research" in humanities-type subjects.
  - Individual differences and motivation are largely ignored.

### Strength of evidence

- **Strong** for worked examples and expertise reversal in technical domains [32].
- **Untested in the sources opened** for L2 grammar and reading specifically.
- The redundancy versus captions question for L2 listening could not be resolved from opened sources (see Limitations).

### What it means for Lucid

- Teach Pre-A1 to A2 grammar with **fully worked examples** (a Hebrew explanation integrated next to the English example), then fade the support.
- Put **glosses inline** with the word or sentence, not in a separate panel.
- **Reduce scaffolding by level**, because of expertise reversal.
- Treat any audio-plus-identical-text presentation as an **A/B test**, not a default.

## 7. Desirable difficulties and learning versus performance

### Evidence summary

- **Performance in training is not learning.** Soderstrom & Bjork's (2015) integrative review argues that "performance during acquisition" is an unreliable index of long-term learning [33]:
  - improvements in performance can fail to produce learning;
  - learners often mistake their in-training performance for durable learning [33].
- **Concrete desirable difficulties with L2 or general support:**
  - spacing [1][2][4];
  - retrieval instead of restudy [15][19];
  - generation and errorful attempts before the answer [21];
  - interleaving of confusable categories [27][28][29].
- **Interleaving looks worse during practice.** In Nakata & Suzuki, interleaving produced the most errors during training yet the best delayed retention [28].
- **Difficulty is not desirable for everyone.** Serrano (2022) reports the L2 "desirable difficulty framework" of Suzuki, Nakata & DeKeyser (2019): the best difficulty depends jointly on the language feature, the learner and the practice condition [4].
  - A longer lag helped higher-proficiency learners and those who coped well during training.
  - The opposite held for learners already struggling [4].
- **Retrieval needs a minimum success rate.** Retrieval at ≤ 50% success without feedback produced no benefit [15].

### Strength of evidence

- **Strong** that in-session accuracy misleads [33][19][28].
- **Moderate** that difficulty must be calibrated to the learner [4][15].

### What it means for Lucid

- Measure success by **delayed** retention probes (≥ 1 day, ideally ≥ 7 days), not session accuracy.
- Calibrate difficulty per learner. Struggling learners get shorter lags and more support.

## 8. Long-term retention

### Evidence summary

- **Spacing over long intervals (strong).** Optimal gaps scale with the retention interval [1][2]. For a one-year retention interval, the optimal gap was roughly 5–10% of the year, about three to five weeks. Spacing study over at least several months "seems likely to produce a highly favorable return", potentially doubling what is remembered for the same study time [2].
- **Test-based benefits last longer (strong).** Testing effects are larger at longer retention intervals [15][18].
- **Intentional vocabulary gains decay (strong, L2).** Gains from intentional vocabulary activities drop considerably at delayed posttests [23].
- **Long-term memory is modelled by the scheduler.** In FSRS, stability gains are largest when recall probability has dropped before review, which encodes the long-term spacing benefit [9].
- **Interval caps cost workload.** Anki sets a 100-year maximum interval by default. Lowering it raises workload [7].

### Strength of evidence

- **Strong** for general memory.
- **Limited** for multi-year L2 vocabulary: Bahrick's four-participant study is described in [2].

### What it means for Lucid

- Treat retention as a **multi-month programme**. Core items stay in the scheduler indefinitely.
- Analytics should report **retention at 30 and 90 days or more**, not just streaks.

## 9. Vocabulary acquisition

### 9.1 Number of encounters

- **Webb (2007).** 121 Japanese learners met words 1, 3, 7 or 10 times, with ten tests of different aspects of word knowledge [25]:
  - every increase in repetitions improved at least one aspect of knowledge;
  - **10 encounters** in context gave substantial learning;
  - full knowledge may need **more than 10** [25].
- **Uchihara et al. (2019).** A meta-analysis of 26 studies (45 effect sizes, N = 1,918) found a **medium correlation, r = .34**, between number of encounters and incidental vocabulary learning [24].
- **Yanagisawa & Webb (2022).** A meta-analysis of 42 studies (N = 4,628) found [26]:
  - each additional encounter added **9.4%** to immediate learning;
  - activities requiring the learner to use the word in their **own composition** were associated with +23.3% (immediate) and +21.0% (delayed) gains;
  - recognition tests yielded 22.5% higher measured gains than receptive recall tests. **How a word is tested changes how much appears to be learned** [26].

### 9.2 Incidental versus intentional learning

- **Intentional activities.** Flashcards, lists, writing and fill-in-the-blanks produce real but **decaying** gains [23].
- **Incidental learning.** Learning from input depends on repetition [24] and on task involvement [26]:
  - The Involvement Load Hypothesis was largely supported.
  - "Need" and "evaluation" mattered.
  - "Search" did not reach significance [26].
- **Combined.** The two are complementary. Deliberate scheduling secures high-frequency words. Input gives the many extra encounters needed for depth [25][24].

### 9.3 Lexical coverage for comprehension

- **Hu & Nation (2000)**, as reported by Nation (2006) [36][38]:
  - with a fiction text at 80% coverage, no reader comprehended adequately;
  - at 95%, adequate comprehenders were still a minority;
  - a regression implied **98%** coverage (one unknown word in 50) is needed for most learners.
- **The 98% figure is contested.** A 2023 replication by Kremmel et al. "could not fully" reproduce the original results [39].
- **Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski (2010)** proposed two thresholds [37]:
  - **optimal**: 98% coverage, about **8,000 word families**;
  - **minimal**: 95% coverage, about **4,000–5,000 families**;
  - small increments of vocabulary help comprehension even when coverage barely changes.
- **Nation (2006)** [36]:
  - 98% coverage requires **8,000–9,000 word families for written text** and **6,000–7,000 for spoken text**;
  - about 4,000 families plus proper nouns gives about 95% coverage of novels and newspapers;
  - well-educated native speakers know about 20,000 families.
- **Listening.** van Zeeland & Schmitt (described in [40]) suggest **95% coverage may suffice for listening** to informal narratives.
  - Mean comprehension scores were 9.62, 8.22, 7.65 and 7.35 out of 10 at 100%, 98%, 95% and 90% coverage.
  - The abstract of [40] links 95% to roughly **2,000–3,000 word families**.
  - Schmitt et al. stress that Nation's figures come from text profiling, not learner testing, and need checking against larger corpora [40].

### 9.4 Vocabulary size targets per CEFR level

The two main sources measure in different units, so their numbers are not directly comparable. Milton reports **lemmas among the 5,000 most frequent**, measured with the XLex test [34]. Nation reports **word families** [35].

| CEFR | CEFR vocabulary-range descriptor (2001, via [34]) | Milton: XLex band, lemmas out of the top 5,000 [34] | Nation's suggestion [35] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-A1 | (not in the 2001 scale) | — | — |
| A1 | "basic vocabulary repertoire of isolated words and phrases" | < 1,500 | ~120 survival words and phrases |
| A2 | "sufficient vocabulary … basic communicative needs" | 1,500–2,500 | most frequent 1,000 families |
| B1 | "sufficient vocabulary … most topics pertinent to everyday life" | 2,750–3,250 (2,500–3,250 in Milton & Alexiou 2020, via [35]) | 2,000–3,000 most frequent |
| B2 | "good range of vocabulary … most general topics" | 3,250–3,750 | ~4,000 (2,000–3,000 high-frequency plus 1,000–2,000 technical); "Oxford 3000 (B2)" |
| C1 | "broad lexical repertoire … idiomatic expressions" | 3,750–4,500 | 5,000–6,000; "Oxford 5000 (B2–C1)" |
| C2 | "very broad lexical repertoire … connotative levels" | 4,500–5,000 | 7,000–9,000 |

- **Milton & Alexiou's data.** Data from over 500 learners in several countries showed vocabulary size explaining about **60–70% of variance in CEFR level**, except the Hungarian data [34]. Groups overlapped, samples were small, and the figures differed between languages [34].
- **The two scholars disagree at higher levels.** Nation notes his B2–C2 sizes are "largely greater" than Milton's [35]. Laufer (2020, cited in [35]) suggests B1 involves about 4,000 words.

### What it means for Lucid

- Use a **frequency-ordered core**: NGSL, with NGSL-Spoken for Real-Life English and NAWL for academic C1.
- Track **lemmas with family links**.
- **Gate reading and listening texts by each learner's estimated coverage.**
- Count incidental encounters toward each word's exposure total.
- Use **composition-level tasks** (Writing Lab, roleplay) to consolidate target words.

## 10. Scheduling a ~35-minute daily session

### Evidence summary

No opened source tests a "35-minute" session specifically, so the structure below is **derived** from the principles above. Each block is linked to its evidence and is meant to be A/B tested.

- **Daily beats weekly; distribution across days beats one long session.**
  - Spacing across sessions beats a single session for L2 vocabulary [4].
  - At programme level, very low weekly doses are insufficient [4].
  - 35 minutes a day is about 4 hours a week, above the one-to-two-hour "drip-feed" level criticised in [4], but far below intensive programmes.
- **Within a session:**
  - present the whole set of new items before repeating any item [4];
  - put the second retrieval of new items after a delay inside the session [4][22];
  - give feedback on every error [20][15].
- **Order of review items.** Mixed or blocked order hardly mattered for the size of the testing effect [15].
- **Workload control is necessary.** Higher retention targets increase workload steeply [7].
- **Commercial app evidence is thin.** In a semester-long Duolingo case study of 9 learners, time on app correlated moderately with gains, but motivation varied and learners were frustrated with the materials [45].

### Proposed default session (design, for testing)

| # | Block | Default length | Content | Evidence link |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Warm-up recall | 3 min | 5–8 items first learned 1–3 days ago; cued recall plus feedback | Testing effect larger at ≥ 1-day retention intervals [15]; spacing [2] |
| 2 | Due reviews (FSRS) | 10 min (soft cap) | All due vocabulary, chunk and grammar cards in shuffled order; objective grading | High-utility techniques [5]; FSRS scheduling [7][9]; order has little impact [15] |
| 3 | New learning | 10 min | 1–3 "try-first" questions → worked examples (grammar) → guided practice; default **8 new lexical items**; one full pass of the new set before any repeats | Errorful generation [21]; worked examples [32]; within-session spacing [4] |
| 4 | Use it | 8 min | Short roleplay or writing task that requires today's and recent target words; feedback on errors; blocked repetition of the same speaking task at most 2–3 times | Composition-level use [26]; fluency practice benefits from blocking but massed repetition is double-edged [4] |
| 5 | Exit check | 4 min | Delayed second recall of today's new items, plus a predict-then-check confidence prompt | Number of within-session retrievals [22]; metacognitive miscalibration [19][33] |

The **load governor** that adjusts block 3 is described in the decisions below (D3).

## Implementation decisions for Lucid

Each decision gives a default, the evidence behind it, and how to test it. "Design choice" marks parameters with no direct empirical value in the sources. They are reasoned defaults to be tuned by A/B tests.

**Scheduler (SRS)**

- **D1. Scheduler.** Use FSRS through `ts-fsrs` (MIT), pinned to a specific version. Use its default FSRS version only if it is in a stable release; otherwise use FSRS-6 [12][10]. Store every review as `{item_id, card_type, ts, rating, latency_ms, exercise_type, hint_used}` so the model can be refitted or replaced [10].
  - *Test:* calibration (log loss and RMSE) of predicted versus observed recall on Lucid's own data, recomputed monthly.
- **D2. Retention target.** Default desired retention is **0.90** [7].
  - Learners may choose "Light" at 0.85 or "Exam" at 0.95. Never allow above 0.97 [7].
  - *Test:* observed pass rate on due reviews should sit within ±3 percentage points of the target.
- **D3. Personal parameters and the new-item governor.**
  - Use default FSRS parameters until a learner has **≥ 1,000 graded reviews**. This threshold is a design choice; Anki warns about poor fits under a few hundred reviews [7]. After that, refit **monthly** [7].
  - Default **8 new lexical items per day** (allowed range 0–15). The figure is design arithmetic: 2,809 NGSL words at 8 a day ≈ 351 days, which reaches roughly Milton's B1 band [34] in about a year.
  - Cut new items to 4 when projected reviews exceed 12 minutes a day over the next 7 days. Allow up to 15 when projected reviews are under 6 minutes.
  - *Test:* 30-day retention and churn at 6 versus 8 versus 12 new items a day.
- **D4. Objective grading, not self-rating buttons.**
  - Wrong → **Again**.
  - Correct after a hint or above the learner's 80th-percentile latency → **Hard**.
  - Correct → **Good**.
  - **Easy** only when the answer is correct, fast, and the card has passed at least twice.
  - "Hard" is never assigned to a failure [7].
  - *Test:* calibration as in D1.
- **D5. Learning steps.** A new card gets its first exposure, a spaced retrieval later in the same session, and the exit-check retrieval. After that, FSRS takes over. Same-day steps are kept few and under one day [7][22].
  - *Test:* 2 versus 4 same-day retrievals, measured by 7-day recall per minute spent [22].
- **D6. Maximum interval.** Cap intervals at **365 days** for the most frequent 3,000 lemmas (production cards) and **3,650 days** otherwise. This is a design choice. The cost is extra workload [7]; overly long gaps cost less than overly short ones [2].

**Retrieval and feedback**

- **D7. Retrieval format ladder per lexical item.**
  1. Recognition is used only for the first exposure.
  2. The item then moves to **meaning recall** (English → Hebrew).
  3. **Form recall** (Hebrew → English, typed or spoken) is a separate FSRS card. It unlocks when the meaning-recall card reaches stability of at least 7 days (design choice).
  4. Then **use in a sentence**.

  Evidence: cued recall beats recognition (g = 0.61 versus 0.29) [15]; direction of learning moderates outcomes [23].
  - *Test:* report retention **by test format** separately, because format inflates or deflates measured gains [26].
- **D8. Feedback.** Show the correct answer on **every** error, immediately [20][15]. On correct answers, show only minimal confirmation, since feedback after correct responses adds little [20]. **Never** present unscored retrieval sets [15].
- **D9. "Try-first" items.** Ask up to 3 generation or prediction questions before each new grammar or lexical explanation, always followed by the answer. They are not counted toward mastery [21].
- **D10. No skipping on self-judgement.** "I already know this" triggers a quick recall check. Two correct recalls on separate days set a high initial stability; one failure reverts the card to normal learning [19][33].

**Interleaving, lags and fluency**

- **D11. Grammar sequence.**
  - The first exposure is **blocked**: worked examples, then practice on that structure only.
  - From the next session on, the review set **interleaves** each structure with 1–2 competing structures chosen by the curriculum team (for example, past simple versus present perfect) [28][29][4].
  - *Test:* 7-day delayed accuracy, hybrid versus blocked-only.
- **D12. Vocabulary order.** Shuffle due vocabulary reviews (order has little impact) [15]. Do not use category-interleaving to *teach* new words [27].
- **D13. Lags by proficiency.**
  - Pre-A1 to A2 grammar points are re-practised after **1–3 days**.
  - B2 and above use **5–7 days**.
  - Lags adapt from accuracy during training: if a learner struggles, shorten the lag [4]. FSRS still governs individual cards.
- **D14. Fluency and pronunciation.**
  - Allow immediate repetition of the same AI roleplay or pronunciation task **2–3 times** (blocked) [4].
  - Do not loop the same task six or more times in a row: massed repetition was "double-edged" [4].

**Cognitive load**

- **D15. Grammar presentation.**
  - At Pre-A1 to A2, every new grammar point has **≥ 2 worked examples**, with the Hebrew explanation physically adjacent to each English example (no separate panel) [32].
  - Hebrew scaffolding fades with level: full at Pre-A1 and A1, on demand at B1, off by default at C1 [32].
  - *Test:* immediate and 7-day accuracy, faded versus constant support.
- **D16. Redundancy.** Avoid narration that repeats on-screen text word for word in explanations [32]. Reading-while-listening for comprehension is an **A/B-tested option**, not a default, because the L2 evidence was not verified.

**Input, coverage and lists**

- **D17. Coverage gating.** Estimate each learner's known-word set: words with FSRS retrievability ≥ 0.9, plus placement-test estimates. Compute coverage for every text and audio clip.
  - **≥ 98%** → "read freely".
  - **95–98%** → "with glosses".
  - **< 95%** → hidden or flagged "challenge".
  - For **listening, 95%** is the minimum for "listen freely".

  Thresholds come from [36][37][40] and are treated as tunable, since the 98% figure only partly replicated [39].
  - *Test:* comprehension-check score versus coverage band.
- **D18. Encounter counting.** Log every incidental encounter of a target lemma in reading, listening, roleplay or AI-tutor text. Surface items with **fewer than 10 total encounters** for extra exposure [25][24][26].
- **D19. Composition-level use.** Writing Lab and roleplay briefs each name 3–5 target items from the learner's recent cards. Use of them is checked and fed back into the scheduler as a successful review [26].
- **D20. Vocabulary targets per level.** These are receptive exit targets in lemmas, a design choice built on [34][35]:

  | Level | Exit target | Notes |
  |---|---|---|
  | Pre-A1 | 300 | survival words and chunks |
  | A1 | 1,000 | |
  | A2 | 2,000 | |
  | B1 | 3,000 | about all of NGSL 1.2 |
  | B2 | 3,750 | plus the start of NAWL |
  | C1 | 4,500 of the top 5,000 | plus NAWL 957 |
  | C2 | 5,000 of the top 5,000 | aiming for 8,000+ families overall through reading [36][37] |

  Re-estimate these with Lucid's placement data.
- **D21. Lists and licences.**
  - Core spine: NGSL 1.2, NGSL-Spoken and NAWL 1.2, all CC BY-SA 4.0 [41a][41b][41c].
  - Keep the derived lexicon with Hebrew glosses in a **separate data file** released under **CC BY-SA 4.0**, with attribution in an in-app Credits screen [42]. Get legal review before shipping.
  - Use AWL only with written permission from the rights holder [43].
  - Do not ingest Oxford 3000/5000 or EVP data without a licence (status unverified).

**Measurement**

- **D22. Delayed retention as the success metric.** Insert **delayed probe items** at 1, 7 and 30 days or more after first learning (≤ 5% of review volume). Report "retained at 30 days" as the primary learning KPI rather than in-session accuracy or streaks [33][15][2].
- **D23. Daily session template.** Implement the five-block, 35-minute session in §10 with the D3 governor.
  - *Test:* the template versus a "reviews-only first" variant, measured by 30-day retention per minute and 4-week retention of learners.

## What not to build

1. **No home-grown or SM-2 scheduler as the primary engine.** SM-2 cannot target a retention level, and trainable models predict recall better [6][10][11].
2. **No "mark as known"/"skip" without a recall check.** Learners' predictions do not track their actual recall [19].
3. **No retrieval without feedback.** Silent quiz streams at low success rates give no benefit [15][20].
4. **No reread-the-list or highlight study modes.** They are rated low utility [5].
5. **No unlimited new items a day.** This leads to review debt; see D3 and [7].
6. **No retention target above 0.97 and no "100% mastery" promise** [7].
7. **No four-button Anki-style self-rating** for Pre-A1 to B1 learners. Self-ratings misuse "Hard" and inflate intervals [7].
8. **No category-interleaving to *teach* new words** [27]. No full interleaving at a novice's very first exposure to a grammar point [4][28].
9. **No cramming mode as the default** [2][4]. If an exam-cram mode exists, label it as short-term.
10. **No in-session accuracy as the mastery metric** [33][28].
11. **No separate-panel glosses or word-for-word narration of on-screen text** in explanations [32].
12. **No worked-example-heavy lessons for advanced learners** [32].
13. **No unassisted reading or listening below 95% estimated coverage** [36][37][40].
14. **No Oxford 3000/5000, EVP or AWL data in the product without a licence.** NGSL-family adaptations must remain CC BY-SA [41a][42][43].
15. **No "deliberate practice" or "10,000 hours" marketing claims** [30].
